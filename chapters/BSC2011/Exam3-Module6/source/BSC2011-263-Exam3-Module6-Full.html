Conservation Biology

Conservation biology is an integrated science that draws on many branches of biology to sustain biological diversity at every level. It examines whatever causes biodiversity to persist, decline, or recover.

Some people call conservation biology a "discipline with a deadline" because the need for remedial action is absolutely urgent for many species threatened by extinction. For example, conservation biologists in Africa are concerned about the rapidly diminishing population of lions in Sub-Saharan Africa. Other threatened species include short-beaked dolphins, leatherback turtles, and forest elephants.

Conservation ecologists are concerned about many types of diversity, including species diversity, genetic diversity, and ecosystem diversity. Genetic diversity is a major issue for conservation biologists because when populations dwindle and genetic diversity falls, extinction is more likely.

Genetic diversity comes down to alleles—the alternative versions of a gene. The more alleles a population holds, the better its odds that some individuals can withstand a new disease, a shift in climate, or an altered habitat. Once an allele disappears, only a new mutation or an immigrant can bring it back. Our food supply shows the danger: roughly 95% of the apple varieties grown in the United States in 1900 no longer exist, and in 1970 a single fungus devastated the U.S. corn crop because most fields were genetically too similar to resist it.

Small, isolated populations are especially prone to extinction for several reasons: genetic drift (alleles vanish by chance), inbreeding depression (the offspring of related parents survive and reproduce poorly), a small effective population size (discussed below), chance (stochastic) events such as a storm or a disease outbreak, greater vulnerability to disease, and too little genetic variation to adapt to change.

A well-known example of genetic rescue involves the Florida panther population. By the 1990s, the population had declined to only about 20–30 individuals and was suffering from severe inbreeding, which led to genetic defects and reduced survival. To address this, conservationists introduced eight female pumas from Texas into the population in 1995. This increased gene flow and genetic diversity, reducing the effects of inbreeding depression and improving overall population health. As a result, the population has rebounded to about 200 individuals, demonstrating how introducing new individuals can restore genetic variation in small, isolated populations.

Ecology in the News: Relocated Lynx and a Lethal Gene

Moving animals does not always rescue a population. According to a study published in November 2026, a new Eurasian lynx population in northeastern Switzerland was started in the early 2000s with just 12 animals, three of which were healthy carriers of a harmful recessive allele of a gene called FGF23. With so few founders, genetic drift and inbreeding pushed the share of carriers from roughly 10% in the source population to about half in the new one. Lynx that inherit two copies of the allele die young as minerals build up in their organs.

Unlike the Florida panther rescue, which added alleles, this relocation concentrated a damaging one—a reminder that founder groups should be large and genetically varied. A separate project in Slovenia and Croatia is doing just that, bringing in 18 lynx from the Carpathian Mountains to add new genes to an inbred population.

Pablo Escobar's Hippos

In the 1980s, Colombian drug lord Pablo Escobar illegally imported four hippos to his private zoo at Hacienda Nápoles. After Escobar's death in 1993, most of the animals were relocated, but the hippos were left behind due to the difficulty of moving them. The small founding population escaped into the surrounding river systems and has since grown to over 100 individuals.

This example highlights a major issue with small founding populations: low genetic diversity and inbreeding. Because the population originated from only four individuals, many of the descendants are closely related, increasing the likelihood of inbreeding depression and the accumulation of harmful genetic traits over time. Even though the population has grown in size, its genetic health may still be compromised because it was founded by so few individuals.

In addition, hippos are considered ecosystem engineers, meaning their presence can significantly alter the structure and function of the ecosystems they invade, contributing to ecological disruption.

Major Threats to Biodiversity

Scientists don't really know how much biodiversity has been lost in recent years, but the numbers are likely high. One estimate is that animal populations have decreased by about half in the last 48 years—a staggering figure.

The rate of extinction in recent decades has been as much as 1,000 times greater than the "natural" (background) rate of extinction that has occurred over the history of the Earth. The rate of extinction is so high today, that many scientists are referring to the current loss of species as the "sixth mass extinction."

The following are some of the major threats to biodiversity that conservation biologists have to deal with:

  • Habitat destruction – Habitat destruction (habitat loss) is the most important threat to biodiversity. Humans have already degraded approximately 40% to 50% of Earth's terrestrial habitats. One estimate is that we lose an acre of rainforest every 2 minutes!

  • Invasive and introduced species – Invasive (introduced) species are organisms that are moved—intentionally or unintentionally—by humans into regions where they did not previously exist. These species often lack natural predators in their new environment and can spread rapidly, outcompeting native species, disrupting food webs, and altering ecosystem structure and function. For example, zebra mussels reached the Great Lakes by accident and now smother native mussels and encrust pipes, docks, and boats.

    A well-known example of an invasive species is the Burmese python in the Florida Everglades. These snakes were introduced through the pet trade and have established large populations in the wild. As apex predators, they have dramatically reduced populations of native small mammals and other species, disrupting food webs and ecosystem balance.

    The extinction of the dodo is often mistakenly attributed to the species being unintelligent or poorly adapted. In reality, dodos were well adapted to their island environment, which lacked natural predators. Their extinction was primarily caused by the introduction of invasive species (such as rats, pigs, and monkeys) by humans, which preyed on their eggs and competed for resources. This example illustrates how species that evolve in isolated environments can be especially vulnerable to sudden ecological changes caused by human activity.

  • Overexploitation – Also called overharvesting. For example, the overfishing of tuna is an overexploitation of our resources, and about 14% of fisheries have collapsed. Another example: Ivory hunters have overexploited the elephant populations in Africa.

  • Pollution – As human populations have grown exponentially, our use of fossil fuels has led to a significant amount of air pollution. This may disrupt nutrient cycles and cause extinctions at very high rates. In addition, human use of DDT (a pesticide) polluted ecosystems and impacted entire food webs. DDT pushed the bald eagle toward extinction; Rachel Carson's book Silent Spring publicized the damage, and the public reaction helped lead to the creation of the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Finally, the drugs we take are ultimately excreted and can pollute waterways.

  • Climate change – Human activities have also contributed to global climate change—specifically, global warming. This is a problem for species that cannot move to a new home range fast enough to survive. So far, few extinctions can be attributed to climate change with certainty; the open question is whether species can adapt as quickly as temperatures climb.

    A key issue with modern climate change is not just that conditions are changing, but that they are changing at an unusually rapid rate. Over much of Earth's recent history (the last ~10,000 years), climate conditions were relatively stable, which allowed human civilization and many ecosystems to develop under predictable environmental conditions. In contrast, current increases in atmospheric carbon dioxide are driving rapid shifts in temperature and precipitation patterns. Because evolutionary adaptation typically occurs over much longer timescales, many species may not be able to adjust quickly enough, increasing the risk of population declines and extinction.

Case Study: Cheetahs

Cheetahs (species Acinonyx jubatus) were once widespread across Africa and Asia, but human actions have reduced their populations by more than 90% since 1900. Today's cheetah population has low genetic diversity, which poses a problem for conservationists. The global cheetah population, which is now found almost entirely in Africa (with a tiny remnant in Iran), is now only about 7,100 individuals.

This is just a handful of relevant facts that a conservation biologist might want to know when developing a plan to save the cheetahs. Some other important questions are as follows:

  • What are cheetahs' interactions with other species?
  • What organisms do cheetahs compete with?
  • Where do cheetahs live?
  • What is the size of cheetahs' home range?
  • What do cheetahs eat?
  • What are cheetahs' life history traits?
  • Why do cheetahs die?
  • What are cheetahs' social systems?
  • What are human attitudes about cheetahs?
  • What diseases are cheetahs particularly susceptible to, and how are those diseases transmitted to populations of cheetahs?
  • Is the population of cheetahs genetically diverse?
  • What are the age structure and sex ratio of the population?
  • How fast do cheetah populations grow?
  • What are the chances of extinction?

In reality, we don't know the answers to many—if not all—of these questions. This demonstrates that conservation biology is a "crisis science."

Species that are the targets of conservation efforts, such as the cheetah, tend to share several issues or problems. Biological issues relating to conservation ecology include the need to preserve diversity, the problems that arise in small populations, and the impact of competition.

Small population problems often include the genetic changes that result from genetic drift. In a small population, even a minor change in the gene pool can have a large cumulative impact. In addition, competition may limit the species' ability to rebound from a bottleneck because it could limit the resources available to the species. Through an understanding of these biological issues relating to conservation ecology, biologists can better direct their efforts toward saving endangered and threatened species.

Global cheetah populations have low genetic diversity. (Researchers discovered this when they tried to do a skin transplant between an East African cheetah and a South African cheetah, and the skin was not rejected.) The cause is a population bottleneck late in the Pleistocene, when a warmer, drier climate shrank the cheetah's range; numbers later recovered, but the lost alleles did not. Low genetic diversity can lead to inbreeding depression, low sperm counts, low sperm motility, low fertility in females, and poor survivorship of offspring. It also reduces a species' evolutionary potential to deal with environmental changes.

A real-world analogy of inbreeding can be seen in historical royal families, as well as in fictional examples like Game of Thrones, where repeated intermarriage among close relatives leads to the expression of harmful genetic traits due to reduced genetic diversity.

Another problem facing cheetah populations is the "supermom" phenomenon, in which a small number of females produce a disproportionately large share of offspring. In the southern Serengeti, a single mother was responsible for 10% of all the cheetahs living there. This creates a skew in reproductive success, meaning that fewer individuals are contributing genes to the next generation. As a result, the effective population size is reduced, even if the total population size appears larger. This loss of genetic representation can decrease genetic diversity and increase the risk of inbreeding and genetic drift over time.

Another problem facing cheetahs is competition with lions and hyenas, which kill many cubs; as many as 95% of cheetah cubs die. Finally, the wildlife trade (legal and illegal) is another threat to cheetah populations. Dr. Dutton mentioned that it is popular to own cheetahs as pets in Saudi Arabia.

Effective Population Size

In any given population, not all individuals will breed. That is, there will be some individuals who—for whatever reason—never pass on their genes to future generations. For example, some species have a small number of extremely territorial males that breed with many females and a large number of "weaker" males who don't breed at all. Moreover, sex ratios in any given population may be skewed. For example, there are generally more female deer than male deer on the Earth because hunters specifically hunt large male deer.

Given these qualifications, ecologists need to determine the effective population size—a measure of how many individuals are, in effect, sharing equally in passing genes on to the next generation. Importantly, this depends only on breeding individuals and their relative proportions, not the total population size. When reproduction is uneven or sex ratios are skewed, the effective population size can be much smaller than the actual population, reducing genetic diversity and increasing the effects of genetic drift. A past bottleneck lowers the effective population size as well.

The general equation for effective population size is as follows:

\[ \text{Effective Population Size } (N_e) = \frac{4N_mN_f}{N_m + N_f} \]

where . . .

  • \(N_m\)= The number of breeding males in the population
  • \(N_f\)= The number of breeding females in the population

So, for example, let's say that we have a population of 16 cheetahs, in which there are five breeding males and two breeding females. The effective population size could be calculated as follows:

\[ \text{Effective Population Size} = \frac{4N_mN_f}{N_m + N_f} = \frac{4(5)(2)}{5 + 2} = \frac{40}{7} = 5.71 \]

The effective population size will be equal to the actual population size if there is an equal number of males and females, all of which are breeding. So, for example, if the population of 16 cheetahs consists of eight breeding males and eight breeding females, the effective population size could be calculated as follows:

\[ \text{Effective Population Size} = \frac{4N_mN_f}{N_m + N_f} = \frac{4(8)(8)}{8 + 8} = \frac{256}{16} = 16 \]

An extreme skew sets a hard ceiling: with just one breeding male, the effective population size stays below 4 regardless of how many females breed.

Because only a limited portion of the population is actually contributing genes to the next generation, the effective population size is much smaller than the census size of 200. A smaller effective population size increases the effects of genetic drift, which can reduce genetic variation over time and increase the risk of inbreeding. Even if the total headcount seems fairly large, unequal reproductive success can make the population genetically fragile. For instance, if the other 170 panthers were all breeding females, the effective population size would be only about 102.

Metapopulations

Many organisms live in habitat patches separated by unsuitable environments. Therefore, there may be many subpopulations linked together by dispersal. Collectively, the group of these subpopulations linked by dispersal is called a metapopulation. For example, there are 33 cheetah subpopulations remaining in southern Africa; these subpopulations are separated by unsuitable environments. Some of them are close enough that cheetahs can move from one to another, resulting in gene flow. Collectively, these subpopulations make up a metapopulation.

Because individuals move in and out, each subpopulation is an open population. Open populations are described by the BIDE model (births, immigration, deaths, and emigration) rather than by the BD model (births and deaths only), which assumes a closed population.

Two kinds of subpopulations found within a metapopulation are as follows:

  • A source population is one in which births outnumber deaths, so the per capita growth rate is positive and the population is growing.
  • A sink population is one in which deaths outnumber births, so the per capita growth rate is negative and the population is shrinking.

Subpopulations within a metapopulation can experience colonization and extinction events. The rate of colonization is primarily dependent on the distance of the subpopulation from a source, and the rate of extinction is primarily dependent on the size of the subpopulation's habitat.

Ideally, ecologists could find ways to promote dispersal of individuals from source populations to sink populations in order to keep those populations from dwindling and eventually disappearing.

Sink populations survive only because immigrants keep arriving from sources, so when resources are limited, protecting source habitat is usually the priority. A sink that humans created can sometimes be turned back into a source by fixing the cause. However, protecting poor habitat that can never support a self-sustaining population creates an ecological trap: animals keep moving in, only to die.

Two Approaches to Conservation

There are two main approaches to conservation:

  • A species-specific approach – This approach attempts to save individual species from extinction. For example, governments have taken this approach to save the black rhino, the manatee, and the black-footed ferret, which are all threatened with extinction. The Endangered Species Act (ESA) and the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES) embrace this approach.

    The species-specific approach to conservation is popular among donors because they feel a certain connection to charismatic species, such as cheetahs or koalas. The problem with this approach is that many of the species that need the most help are not charismatic. For example, it is relatively easy to get people to donate money for cheetah conservation, but it is much harder to get people to donate money for the conservation of insects or forests.

  • A system-level approach – This approach does not limit conservation efforts to specific species; rather, it attempts to save all of the species within a given ecosystem. For example, governments may preserve entire sections of the Everglades or the Afromontane forests to preserve all of the species that live there. This is a more recent strategy, which has been adopted by The Nature Conservancy (TNC).

    The benefit of this approach is that many species converge, and saving entire ecosystems preserves many species instead of just one. However, this approach is difficult to implement because it is difficult to "sell" to the public. Moreover, some "hotspots" of biodiversity are in countries with governments that are not receptive to conservation efforts. Finally, it is more difficult to gauge the success of a system-level approach than that of a species-specific approach.

The Problem

Conservation biologists face a difficult problem. Consider the following alarming statistics:

  • Almost half of the surface of the terrestrial Earth has been degraded or altered in some way.
  • Extinction rates today are as much as 1,000 times the natural (background) rate. Nearly 25% of mammals, 13% of birds, 18% of fish, and 42% of amphibians are either endangered or threatened. Some scientists refer to this as the sixth mass extinction.

Conservationists are facing some tough choices. Dr. Dutton showed a magazine article entitled, "It's Time to Let Certain Animals Go Extinct," which explains that we are going to have to decide which species to focus on conserving to the exclusion of other species that we will have to let die out. This is sometimes referred to as species triage. This approach has been used in Australia and New Zealand but not yet in the United States. Supporters argue that abandoning expensive, losing battles would free up money to recover more species; critics counter that under triage, no one would have attempted difficult rescues like that of the California condor, which succeeded.

Why Should You Care about Conservation Biology?

Biological diversity is important to humans for the following reasons:

  1. Biological diversity financially benefits humans. Diverse plant and animal life has led to the development of a number of new chemicals, foods, and medicines, which have been commercialized. 70% of the medicines that have been developed in the last 25 years have come from nature. Bioprospecting refers to the search for biological organisms that can be commercialized and used for medical or industrial purposes. For example, the rosy periwinkle is a threatened plant found in Madagascar that produces compounds that can be used to treat leukemia and Hodgkin's lymphoma.

  2. Biological diversity maintains important ecosystem services. Humans depend on ecosystems for clean air, clean water, nitrogen fixation, protection from ultraviolet rays, and flood control. These services are often grouped as provisioning (food, water, fiber, and medicine), regulating (climate, air and water quality, and flood control), and cultural (recreation, beauty, and study). One estimate is that ecosystem services are valued at $44 trillion per year!

  3. Other reasons – Biological diversity is aesthetically pleasing. This explains why people spend so much time looking at Internet pictures of cute animals.

There is good news, too: conservation can work. Florida panthers have climbed from fewer than 30 to about 200, bald eagles from 417 nesting pairs in the lower 48 states (1963) to roughly 71,400 (2020), and California condors from 22 birds (1982) to more than 500. Each recovery required legal protection, funding, and decades of effort.

Quiz

The clearing of 10,000 hectares of tropical rainforest removes a large area of mature forest. Which set of ecological outcomes would a community ecologist most likely expect to occur over the following decades?

a. An increase in primary productivity and a rise in overall species diversity
b. A rapid return to the original forest community within about five years
c. Higher effective population sizes across all remaining species
d. A spread of K-selected species into the disturbed environment
e. Loss of carbon storage, disruption of food webs (trophic downgrading), and weaker regulation of water systems

Removing old-growth forest reduces the ecosystem's ability to store carbon, weakening its role in regulating climate. It also disrupts food webs, often leading to trophic downgrading as top predators and key species are lost. In addition, forests play a major role in stabilizing water cycles, so deforestation leads to poorer watershed regulation. Recovery is typically slow and dominated by early successional species, not a rapid return to the original, complex community.
