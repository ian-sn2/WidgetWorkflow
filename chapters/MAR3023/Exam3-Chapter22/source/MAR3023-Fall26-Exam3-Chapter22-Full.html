Recall that the process of strategy formulation includes planning, implementation, and control. In the last section, we discussed the planning phase. In this segment, we will wrap up marketing strategy formulation by discussing the implementation and control phases.

The Implementation Phase

The implementation phase of the strategic marketing process includes establishing the marketing organization, developing timetables, obtaining and allocating budgets, and executing the marketing program.

Establishing the Marketing Organization

To build an effective marketing organization, companies need clear roles and responsibilities and a smart approach to deploying human resources. Procter & Gamble is a commonly cited example because it has produced an unusually high number of CMOs and CEOs—among the 2,000 largest companies, it ranks first as a starting point for future CMOs and fourth for future CEOs. Their structure shows how major brand functions are divided across key roles, such as:

  • Monitoring the brand (brand managers)
  • Marketing research (understanding the customer)
  • Communications (advertising and promotion)
  • Design (new product development and packaging)

P&G's careers page shows how broad these roles are, with dozens of open marketing positions ranging from customer team marketing to digital marketing technology. Many marketing jobs do not have "marketing" in the title at all.

In most large firms, the brand manager is responsible for the overall marketing effort for a particular brand. This includes making sure the right people are in the right roles at the right time, which depends on strong human resource development. In other words, brand managers need to recruit and motivate employees in ways that support organizational goals.

As competition for talent has intensified, companies now treat recruiting much like consumer marketing. Procter & Gamble is again the model: job candidates knew its brands far better than they knew the company, so its recruiting team began operating like a brand. Effective "employer branding" means presenting the organization as a place top applicants genuinely want to work. About a third of recruiting teams (36%) partner with marketing or communications on this, and another 30% take the lead themselves. The reason is simple: applicants pay close attention to an employer's reputation—about 83% of job seekers check a company's reviews and ratings before applying—and strong employer branding makes it much easier to attract high-quality candidates. Employees who feel aligned with a company's mission and values also tend to be more engaged, which reinforces why talent deployment increasingly involves a marketing perspective.

Some organizations also use structured leadership models to guide how managers support their teams. Google, for example, commits to continual learning for every employee, on the view that capable people can be taught the specifics of a job. Similarly, Microsoft's "Model–Coach–Care" framework encourages leaders to demonstrate the behavior they expect, help employees navigate challenges, and show authentic care for their growth and well-being.

The textbook discusses 2 current issues regarding organization for marketing:

  • The role of the CMO – Many companies now use the title "chief marketing officer" instead of "vice president of marketing." The CMO increasingly serves as the voice of the consumer inside the company and has to keep up with changing consumers at home and abroad, digital and mobile media, and new sources of data and AI for checking decisions. The role's influence has grown: CMOs often oversee budgets above 10% of company revenue, most report to the CEO or COO, and they typically stay in the job about four years.
  • Line versus staff and divisional groupings – A typical business unit has people in both line positions and staff positions.
    • Line positions have authority and responsibility to issue orders to the people who report to them.
    • Staff positions have authority and responsibility to advise people in line positions but cannot issue direct orders to them.

    To carry out strategic plans, organizations rely on several types of divisional groupings:

    • Product line groupings, in which a unit oversees specific product offerings
    • Functional groupings, which align employees by department or business activity
    • Geographical groupings, which divide sales territories by location
    • Market-based groupings, which organize around customer segments; when paired with product line groupings, they form a matrix organization

    Some firms have created the position of category manager to manage an entire product line.

Developing Timetables

Timing can make or break a marketing plan, especially when a company is working under a tight deadline. Dr. Cooke shared an example where Post pitched Honey Bunches of Oats with Greek Yogurt to Walmart and Target while it was still only a concept. The retailers liked the idea so much that they offered six months of free shelf space in exchange for six months of exclusivity, which left Post about half of the year it had planned on. The company still didn't know how to mass-produce dried Greek yogurt or coat the cereal pieces with it, as the retailers wanted. To compress its schedule, Post relied on critical path analysis.

A few tools to assist marketers in designating tasks and developing timetables include action lists, Gantt charts, and PERT charts. Of these, the best tool to use for scheduling a complicated process is critical path analysis using Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT) charts. A sample PERT chart for the development of a new product is presented below:

PERT Chart: New Product Launch

PERT charts might be used, for example, to track the activities involved in a new product launch. Some product launches occur faster than others. PERT charts outline all of the activities that have to take place and how long they are expected to take.

  • The nodes represent the milestones that need to be accomplished.
  • The numbers represent the number of months it takes to get to the next step.
  • The lines represent the tasks that must be done to get from one step to the other.

In the PERT chart above, it takes 5 months to get from activity B (market research) to activity D (developing an ad campaign). Certain tasks have to be completed before others can be undertaken. For example, market research can only be conducted after a prototype has been built. Other tasks can be done "in parallel" (at the same time). For example, market research can be done while building the production facility. Three different paths could be taken to go from prototype to launch:

  • \(A \to B \to D \to F = 12\) months
  • \(A \to B \to E \to F = 11\) months
  • \(A \to C \to E \to F = 15\) months*

The critical path is the longest possible path that will allow us to get from prototype to launch. This determines the minimum time it will take to launch the product.

EXAM TIP: Interpreting PERT Charts

You will likely have an exam question that requires you to interpret a PERT chart.

Here, the critical path is \(A \to C \to E \to F\) because it is the longest possible path from prototype to launch. This path will take 15 months total, which is longer than any other possible path.

Note that you have to complete all the steps in the PERT chart; the critical path simply dictates the steps that will take the longest.

Recall that steps can happen in parallel, so consider that beginning with developing a prototype (activity A), you simultaneously begin market research (activity B) and building your production facility (C). Thus, you could be moving along the path \(A \to B \to D \to F\), which takes 12 months, as well as \(A \to C \to E \to F\), which takes 15 months. Consequently, it will take you 15 months to complete all the necessary steps and be able to launch your product.

It is important to know that \(A \to C \to E \to F\) is the longest, or critical, path because you can only shorten the time it takes to launch your product by reducing the time spent on this path. Even if you were to decrease the time spent developing an ad campaign (moving from activity B to activity D), and thus shorten the path \(A \to B \to D \to F\), it would still take 15 months to build a production facility, redesign the product, and then be ready to launch.

This is the important point about the critical path: The critical path tells us where to focus our efforts when we want to move to the market more quickly. For example, if we are concerned about competitors getting to market first, we should focus on the activities along the critical path: A, C, E, and F. Put differently, the critical path is the bottleneck. The other paths have slack time, so speeding up a step that is not on the critical path only adds slack and does not bring the launch any closer.

Scheduling activities can also be done with a Gantt chart—a graph of a program schedule. For each task, a Gantt chart should specify (1) the task, (2) the person responsible for completing that task, (3) the date to finish the task, and (4) what is to be delivered. As with PERT charts, it is important to distinguish which tasks must be done sequentially and which can be done simultaneously.

Resource Allocation (Budgets)

Share point analysis refers to thinking in terms of the overall market and dividing it up into market share points. A share point is equivalent to 1% of the overall market's sales. This is often used as a common basis of comparison to allocate marketing resources effectively.

The steps for share point analysis are as follows:

  1. Estimate industry sales – This information can be gathered from syndicated research firms or trade publications.
  2. Estimate the firm's market share – The firm's market share is equal to the firm's sales divided by the total industry sales.
  3. Compute gross margin per share point – Gross margin—the contribution to cover fixed costs and profit—is equal to the sales revenue minus the cost of goods sold:

    \[\text{Gross margin} = \text{Sales revenue} - \text{Cost of goods sold}\]

The key decision in resource allocation is how much money the company is willing to spend in additional marketing expenses to increase sales by one share point. Investing in increasing sales by one share point is more profitable if the gross margin per share point is high. Many times, the costs of increasing market share outweigh the benefits. One way to picture the analysis is as two ledgers: an income ledger (gross margin per share point) and an expense ledger (industry marketing spending per share of voice point), linked by the 1.5 rule from competitive parity budgeting. If the added marketing expense is larger than the added gross margin, the investment is not worth making.

Share Point Analysis for Resource Allocation

Suppose that total industry sales are $100 billion, and our firm's sales are $15 billion with $5 billion in gross margin. Our market share is as follows:

\[\text{Market share} = \frac{\$15 \text{ billion}}{\$100 \text{ billion}} = 15\%\]

Therefore, our firm has 15 share points. Now, the gross margin per share point can be computed as follows:

\[\text{Gross margin per share point} = \frac{\text{Gross margin}}{\text{Share points}} = \frac{\$5 \text{ billion}}{15 \text{ share points}} = \$333.3 \text{ million}\]

Now suppose our firm wants to reach 18% market share. If we are able to expand market share by 3 share points, we would gain $1 billion in gross margin ($333.3 million × 3 share points). To get this boost in market share, we need to raise our firm's share of voice based on the 1.5 rule: Increasing market share by 1% requires a 1.5% increase in the share of voice. To increase market share by 3%, we need to increase share of voice by 4.5 voice points (1.5 × 3 share points).

Now, let's assume that marketing expenditures in the industry total $10 billion, meaning each share of voice point costs $100 million. Therefore, increasing the share of voice by 4.5% requires increasing marketing expenditures by $450 million ($100 million per voice point × 4.5 voice points).

Therefore, the return for expanding market share by 3% would be a net gain of $550 million ($1 billion additional gross margin − $450 million additional expenses).

Executing the Marketing Program

Even the best marketing plan fails without strong execution. A marketing strategy outlines how a company will achieve a specific goal, including the target market and the marketing program used to reach it. Marketing tactics are the day-to-day operational decisions that bring the strategy to life and determine whether it succeeds.

Planning vs. Implementation

When performance slips, companies have to figure out whether the problem comes from the plan itself or from how that plan was carried out. The matrix below summarizes the possibilities.

A clear example of poor implementation is Burger King UK's "Women belong in the kitchen" tweet. The campaign was meant to promote a scholarship for female chefs, but the message was posted with no context and immediately went viral for the wrong reasons. Follow-up tweets explained the point—few professional chefs are women—but the backlash had already landed. It's a reminder that even a solid idea can backfire if the execution misses the mark.

A more recent example is Coca-Cola's global brand refresh, which rolled out across roughly 200 markets in the summer of 2026. Many consumers did not notice the change, and the new typeface drew criticism for resembling the one Marlboro uses—an unhelpful association for a product whose health effects are already questioned. A brand refresh is not a bad idea in itself, but the associations it creates need careful thought.

Most firms can easily spot whether something succeeded or failed—what's harder is deciding why. If the planning is flawed, the company needs to go back to the drawing board. If the idea is solid but the rollout is weak, then the focus should be on removing the obstacles that prevented strong implementation.

It's generally better to have a good plan with imperfect execution than a bad plan executed well. Improving implementation is far easier than rebuilding an entire strategy and then re-implementing it. That's why, if a firm can't land in the top-left box of the matrix, the bottom-left is the next best place to be. Planning carries more weight for several reasons: the plan is where the product and its benefits are defined, where goals and the measures of success are set, and where contingency plans are written. A firm that planned well can pivot as soon as results slip, while one that did not has to go back and plan all over again.

Poor planning is easy to find: store shelves with the toothpaste and toothbrush signs swapped, a customer's request to hide what is inside a package printed on the outside of the box, and a football field with its midfield logo painted at the 45-yard line. Either poor planning or poor execution can doom a product.

Improving Implementation

When a company has a solid plan but weak execution, several tactics can help strengthen implementation:

  • Communicate – Strong communication is essential. The marketing plan must be clearly explained to employees and customers, and two-way communication helps employees understand their roles while giving managers useful feedback. Teams should feel safe raising concerns so problems can be solved collectively rather than assigned as blame.
  • Leadership (champions) – A program (or product or project) champion is a person who is able and willing to cut red tape and move the program forward. These individuals can navigate big-picture strategy and day-to-day details, and they persist even when they meet resistance. Steve Jobs is a well-known example. Dr. Cooke emphasized that champions are not a guarantee of success—they push both strong and weak ideas, exist at all levels of an organization, and often support projects that ultimately fail. They usually act on behalf of their own department rather than out of pure enthusiasm for an idea, they back incremental changes as well as radical ones, and they turn up in R&D, production, and other functions, not just marketing. What defines them is determination, not perfect outcomes.
  • Reward performance – Companies can improve implementation by rewarding employees who execute tasks well and help achieve organizational goals, while treating failures as lessons rather than punishing them.
  • Schedule tasks – Marketers need a clear sense of timing and priority. A PERT chart is one tool that maps out tasks and deadlines.
  • Just do it – Avoid "paralysis by analysis," where overthinking prevents action. At some point, additional planning adds little value and slows progress. Being proactive helps move the plan forward.
  • Seek internal and external feedback – Useful feedback comes from customers, channel partners, employees, and other stakeholders.

Overall, communication is the thread that ties these tactics together.

On leadership, Dr. Cooke recommended Stephen R. Covey's The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People and listed the habits without discussing them in class: be proactive; begin with the end in mind; put first things first; think win/win; seek first to understand, then to be understood; synergize; and sharpen the saw.

The Control Phase

The control (evaluation) phase of the marketing process is when managers measure results, spot deviations from the plan, and make adjustments. Management by exception helps them focus on those deviations, understand why they happened, and decide what actions to take. These evaluations include not only financial outcomes but also marketing metrics like customer satisfaction, salesforce motivation, and time-to-market.

If results fall short of goals, managers need to make corrective changes to improve performance. If results exceed expectations, they should capitalize on the momentum and "double down" on what's working.

Measuring Results and Identifying Deviations

Two methods that help a firm measure results and identify deviations from its plan are sales analysis and profitability analysis.

  • Sales analysis involves comparing actual results to sales goals and identifying areas of strength and weakness. For example, a firm that sells nationally can compare sales by region or state; if sales run high in Massachusetts and low in New Mexico, it can shift resources to where they will pay off. The same comparison works across customer segments.
  • Profitability analysis works the same way but focuses on profit instead of sales. It helps firms assess the profitability of products, customer groups, territories, distribution channels, and even order sizes. These insights can guide decisions about whether to expand, maintain, reduce, or eliminate certain offerings or segments.

Marketers should collect sufficient information to control for and detect deviations from the plan before attempting to adjust it.

The Marketing Audit

A marketing audit is a comprehensive, unbiased, periodic review of the strategic marketing process of a firm or strategic business unit. It helps determine a company's marketing return on investment (ROI).

Marketing return on investment (ROI) is a tool for controlling marketing programs that uses current measurement technology to see what marketing spending accomplishes, put numbers on it, and improve it. During the evaluation phase, marketers rely on two tools to strengthen marketing ROI:

  • Marketing metrics are quantified goals, identified ahead of time, that serve as benchmarks against which performance can be judged. Examples include sales goals, retention rates, and sales per square foot.
  • Marketing dashboards present real-time displays of key metrics, making it easy for managers to spot trends or problems quickly.

Together, dashboards and metrics give managers timely insights that support clear, data-driven decisions.

A marketing audit reviews all the activities that make up the marketing effort and evaluates whether they benefit the company and how they can be improved. Audits may be conducted internally, but many firms hire outside consultants to ensure objectivity: they work from the firm's annual plan and are paid whatever they find, so they have no reason to shade the news.

Taking Corrective Action (Contingency Plans)

When there is a deviation between the company's actual performance and its goals, corrective action may need to be taken. Firms commonly have established contingency plans to deal with situations when marketing programs do not work as expected. When the contingency plans in the annual plan were written well, most deviations already have a prepared response, so the firm does not need a major change of course.

Marketing Yourself: You, Inc.

Dr. Cooke closed the segment by applying the strategic marketing process to a job search, treating each student as a brand: "You, Inc." He noted that this material is meant to help with career decisions more than with the exam.

Start with objectives. Money is the obvious one, but most people also want experience, room for advancement, fulfillment, and work-life balance, and the right mix differs from person to person. Next comes a personal mission statement—what you stand for and what you bring—followed by the same steps as any marketing plan: segmentation, targeting, and positioning; situation analysis; strategy; tactics; budget; timing; and a contingency plan. The marketing mix applies as well:

  • Product (you) – Take stock of your strengths and weaknesses, fill gaps with certifications or new skills, and ask a trusted, more experienced friend for an honest "audit." Employers say they look for motivation, oral communication, initiative, maturity, enthusiasm, ambition, assertiveness, self-assurance, problem solving, and written communication. Packaging matters too: clothing, grooming, and demeanor suited to the job you want.
  • Promotion – Advertising is your résumé and cover letters, each tailored to the specific job. Public relations is your social media presence, usually LinkedIn: a professional photo, an "About" section that works as your mission statement, and recommendations. Personal selling is the interview: prepare thoroughly (the preapproach), ask questions to uncover the employer's needs, be ready to handle objections, and follow up with a thank-you note.
  • Place – Flexibility about where you work widens your options.
  • Price – Know the going salary for the job and industry, and adjust what you ask for based on what you do or do not bring.

Everyone has a personal brand, whether or not they manage it. The aim is to be authentic, which means being clear about core values that do not change while staying adaptable. Personal branding is often summarized as three C's: competencies, character, and communication.

As for marketing careers, the two largest sources of entry-level jobs are sales and retailing; others include marketing research, digital marketing, brand management, and newer AI-related roles. Graduate options include a master's in marketing research for analytically minded students, an MBA (a generalist degree that expects several years of work experience and demonstrated leadership) for those aiming at brand management, and a specialized master's in marketing, which concentrates on marketing alone and requires little work experience.
