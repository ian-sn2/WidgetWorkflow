Introduction to the Digestive System

The digestive system consists of the alimentary canal and accessory digestive organs. It plays the following roles in the body:

  • Ingestion – You take in food through the mouth, which is a component of the digestive system.

  • Propulsion – Material has to keep moving along the tube. Propulsion can be voluntary, as in swallowing, or involuntary; the involuntary form is called peristalsis. Dr. Ahlgren pointed out that although the esophagus is usually labeled as a site of peristalsis, its upper (proximal) end is also under voluntary control, because the muscle there is skeletal rather than smooth. This is why something you have just swallowed can sometimes be brought back up, while a pill that has traveled farther down cannot.

  • Digestion – The digestive system breaks food down into smaller nutrients that can be absorbed and utilized by the body. This starts in your mouth and involves both mechanical and chemical processes.

    • Mechanical digestion includes several types of physical breakdown. In addition to chewing in the mouth and churning in the stomach, the intestines perform segmentation—a rhythmic squeezing motion that mixes food with digestive juices and enhances nutrient absorption by repeatedly moving material back and forth. Any breakdown that is physical in nature counts as mechanical, and it happens along the length of the canal, not just in the mouth. Churning presses the stomach's contents against its own walls, and segmentation does similar work in the small intestine. Dr. Ahlgren's analogy: seal mashed potatoes and gravy in a plastic bag and knead it with your hands, and the two blend together.

    • Chemical digestion breaks down food even further through enzymatic activity. Enzymes from the stomach, pancreas, and small intestine split large molecules like carbohydrates, proteins, and fats into smaller units that can pass across the intestinal wall for absorption.

  • Absorption – The body absorbs small nutrients into the blood, which takes those nutrients to parts of the body that need them. Most absorption takes place in the small intestine, although the stomach and large intestine also absorb certain substances. (Fats are routed through lymphatic vessels, which Dr. Ahlgren said will not be a focus.)

  • Elimination – Some of the things that we ingest simply can't be digested and absorbed. We eliminate these things as waste. This process is called defecation, and it is a job of the large intestine.

The two major functional divisions of the digestive system are as follows:

  • The alimentary canal (also called the gastrointestinal tract or the GI tract) is a muscular tube that winds through the body, beginning with the mouth and ending at the anus. It includes the mouth, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, and anus.

    Note that the contents of the alimentary canal are technically outside the body. It is not until nutrients from the food we eat cross the epithelial lining of the canal that they are considered part of the body; from there, they move into blood vessels for distribution.

  • The accessory digestive organs include organs that are involved in digestion but that lie outside of the alimentary canal. These include the teeth and tongue (which are involved in the physical breakdown of food and the propulsion of it down into the GI tract), and various other organs that are involved in the chemical breakdown of foodstuffs (including salivary glands and the gallbladder, liver, and pancreas).

    Dr. Ahlgren emphasized that accessory organs are not part of the alimentary canal itself—they do not form the continuous tube from mouth to anus. Instead, they add materials into the tube to help digestion occur within it. For example, the pancreas releases digestive enzymes into the small intestine, and the salivary glands add saliva into the mouth to aid in swallowing and chemical breakdown. Likewise, the liver is not a tubular organ, but it makes bile and delivers it into the small intestine.

Abdominal Quadrants and Regions

Clinicians divide the abdomen into four quadrants:

  • The right upper quadrant consists of the liver, gallbladder, duodenum, and common bile duct.

  • The left upper quadrant consists of the pancreas, stomach, and pancreatic duct.

  • The right lower quadrant consists of the ascending colon and appendix, as well as the small intestine.

  • The left lower quadrant consists of a part of the large and small intestine.

This provides a useful starting point for an initial assessment of an injury. For example, a patient complaining of a pain in the right lower quadrant probably doesn't have a problem with the stomach or liver because those organs are located in other quadrants. (If they are extremely tender to the touch, a doctor might suspect that they have appendicitis.)

Anatomists further divide the abdomen into nine regions, all of which have names that relate to their relative location. The center region is the umbilical region, which includes the belly button. Above this region is the epigastric region, and below this region is the hypogastric region (also called the pubic region). To the left and right of the epigastric region are the left and right hypochondriac regions. To the left and right of the umbilical regions are the left and right lumbar regions. To the left and right of the hypogastric region are the left and right iliac (inguinal) regions.

The names make sense once you know the landmarks. Hypo- means below and chondr- refers to cartilage, so the hypochondriac regions sit beneath the costal cartilages. Because the diaphragm domes upward, the space tucked under those cartilages belongs to the abdominal cavity, not the thoracic cavity. The lumbar regions are named for the lumbar vertebrae, which lie at the level of the navel if you trace around to the back. Moving straight down from the lumbar regions brings you to the ilia, which gives the iliac regions their name.

Dr. Ahlgren emphasized that students should focus on understanding how to derive the nine abdominal regions visually. If you can picture the 3×3 grid and know the basic landmarks—such as the umbilicus at the center and the costal cartilages above—you can logically determine each region's position.

Remember that these regions include all of the organs within the body; they don't just refer to the superficial bodily surfaces. Because many abdominal organs span more than one region, their locations should be understood approximately rather than as perfectly confined boxes. For example, the gallbladder typically lies at the border between the epigastric and right hypochondriac regions rather than being strictly confined to a single region. Similarly, the cecum (and its attached appendix) is typically located near the border of the lower right abdominal regions rather than being confined neatly within a single region.

Dr. Ahlgren said you do not need to memorize a region-by-region list of organs, but you do need a general sense of where each organ sits. For example, look for the liver as far to the upper right as possible, and for the urinary bladder low and in the center. Practice locating the small intestine, colon, stomach, spleen, and liver.

Clinicians use quadrants to diagnose pain or injury quickly. Anatomists, however, use the nine abdominal regions for more precise organ mapping and structural description. The clinical approach focuses on where symptoms appear, while the anatomical approach focuses on where each organ actually lies. For example, the urinary bladder is found exclusively in the hypogastric (pubic) region under normal conditions; if it extends superiorly into other regions, this indicates pathological overdistension and may constitute a medical emergency.

In general, the alimentary canal wall consists of four layers. From deep to superficial, they are as follows:

  • The mucosa is the innermost layer of the alimentary canal, which lines the lumen. It consists of three layers. From deep to superficial, they are as follows:

    • The innermost lining is made of epithelium that varies along the tract; from the stomach through the intestines, it is simple columnar epithelium. The exceptions are the mouth and esophagus, which are lined with nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium. These regions handle rough, unprocessed food that must be pushed down a narrow tube, so they need a more protective lining. This layer is continuous with the ducts that empty into the lumen of the alimentary canal.

    • Just outside of the epithelium is a layer of areolar (loose) connective tissue called the lamina propria. This layer provides the vasculature necessary for absorption of materials across the alimentary canal wall.

    • The muscularis mucosae is a very thin layer of smooth muscle that makes up the most external portion of the mucosa. Its function is to move the mucosa to dislodge particles that become stuck in the epithelial tissue. Dr. Ahlgren compared this to what happens when you get a popcorn kernel stuck between your gum and tooth—small, hard fragments can sometimes lodge between epithelial cells in the mucosal lining of the GI tract. When this irritation occurs, the muscularis mucosae twitches rhythmically to help dislodge the debris and protect the delicate epithelial surface.

  • The submucosa consists primarily of connective tissue rich in elastic fibers. These fibers allow the wall of the GI tract to both distend as food or fluid fills it and recoil once the material has passed. This stretch-and-return property enables each region of the tract to temporarily expand without permanent deformation. The submucosa also contains larger glands whose secretory portions sit in this layer while their ducts pass through the mucosa to empty onto the luminal surface, along with extensive blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and nerves.

  • The muscularis externa is the outermost layer of muscle in the wall of the alimentary canal. It consists of two layers of smooth muscle: an inner circular layer and an outer longitudinal layer. When the circular layer contracts, the lumen narrows; when the longitudinal layer contracts, the tube shortens. Working together, these alternating contractions create the rhythmic "squeeze, shrink, squeeze, shrink" motion that pushes material forward through the GI tract and helps mix its contents. The circular muscles lie nearest the lumen, while the longitudinal muscles form the outer layer. These two layers are found from the esophagus onward, and their coordinated action is responsible for both peristalsis and segmentation. The stomach adds a third, oblique layer, which is discussed later. In the stomach, this mixing action is called churning, which is essentially segmentation carried out by the stomach.

    Dr. Ahlgren noted that the mucosal epithelium of the GI tract is not ciliated. While cilia might seem helpful for moving food along, they are unnecessary because the thick muscular wall already generates strong propulsive forces. She compared this to the inside of the heart, which also lacks cilia—the heart moves blood efficiently through muscular contraction rather than by surface movement.

  • The serosa is the outermost layer of the alimentary canal wall and corresponds to the visceral layer of the peritoneum. It is part of the same family of serous membranes that includes the pleura (around the lungs) and the pericardium (around the heart). Like other serous membranes, it is composed of simple squamous epithelium supported by a thin layer of connective tissue. Where the visceral peritoneum folds back to meet the parietal peritoneum, it forms a double layer of serous membrane called the mesentery, which suspends digestive organs and provides a passageway for blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerves. Not every organ of the canal is covered by peritoneum. Organs that lie behind the peritoneum are called retroperitoneal (discussed below), and in place of a serosa they have an adventitia, a thicker connective tissue layer like the one seen in blood vessels.

    Dr. Ahlgren emphasized the difference between the two layers of the peritoneum. The visceral peritoneum is the portion that is in direct contact with the organ surfaces, while the parietal peritoneum does not touch the organs and instead lines the inner surface of the body wall. These two layers are continuous with one another, forming a single membrane that folds back on itself to create the mesenteries. By her definition, any peritoneum that does not touch an organ counts as parietal, including the layers that make up the mesenteries.

Mesenteries

Several digestive organs are connected to the body wall by mesenteries, which are formed by a double layer of parietal peritoneum. In class, Dr. Ahlgren emphasized that mesenteries do more than simply anchor the organs—they also suspend them within the abdominal cavity and keep them from sagging downward under gravity. She explained that without these supporting folds, the organs would settle toward the lower abdomen, producing a visible bulge along the body wall, much like the shape of a "spare tire."

To picture how a mesentery forms, imagine the peritoneum wrapping around a section of the gut tube. Vessels and nerves must reach the tube from the back of the body, so the membrane cannot close the loop behind it. Instead, the two sides come together around those vessels and nerves and run back to the body wall as a pair before spreading out along the wall. That paired sheet, with the vessels, nerves, lymphatics, and fat between its layers, is the mesentery. Mesenteries are sheets rather than tubes, and each is named for the organ it attaches to.

Mesenteries serve three important functions:

  • They anchor organs in place.
  • They store adipose.
  • They provide a route for vessels to extend to and from the digestive organs.

Two types of mesenteries are dorsal and ventral mesenteries, which are named based on which side of the abdominal cavity they extend to. A dorsal mesentery (which is the most common type of mesentery) extends from the organ dorsally, to the posterior abdominal wall. A ventral mesentery, on the other hand, extends from the organ ventrally, to the anterior abdominal wall.

Both ventral and dorsal mesenteries originate during embryonic development. As the gut tube (the primitive gut) forms, the peritoneum wraps around it from both sides, and the two layers meet in front and behind the tube. Where the peritoneal layers meet on the front side, they create the ventral mesentery; where they meet on the back side, they form the dorsal mesentery. This developmental folding explains why each region of the digestive tract is suspended from either the anterior or posterior body wall by one of these mesenteries. Dr. Ahlgren said the developmental images do not need to be memorized, since development is not covered in this course. The takeaways are the ventral versus dorsal distinction and the intraperitoneal versus retroperitoneal distinction.

The following are the major dorsal mesenteries:

  • The greater omentum, which connects the inferior aspect of the stomach (the greater curvature) to the abdomen's posterior wall. Dr. Ahlgren stressed how unusual this one is: it is the most ventral structure in the abdominal cavity, yet it is classified as a dorsal mesentery. It hangs from the greater curvature, folds back on itself, and then runs dorsally. It is by far the largest mesentery, resembles a sheet of lace made of fat, drapes over the other organs, and is the first thing seen when the abdomen is opened.
  • The mesentery proper, which is associated with the majority of the small intestine, and attaches to the dorsal body wall.
  • The transverse mesocolon, which is associated with the transverse colon and attaches to the dorsal body wall.
  • The sigmoid mesocolon, which connects the sigmoid colon to the body wall.

The following are the two ventral mesenteries:

  • The falciform ligament, which connects the liver to the anterior abdominal wall and diaphragm. (Note that this mesentery is called a ligament, but it is not the same kind of ligament that connects bone to bone.)
  • The lesser omentum, which connects the liver to the lesser curvature of the stomach and the first part of the duodenum. It suspends the stomach from the underside of the liver.

Together, these two mesenteries anchor the liver and stomach to the ventral body wall near the xiphoid process.

Note that not all digestive organs have a mesentery. Intraperitoneal organs have mesenteries, while retroperitoneal organs do not. The peritoneal cavity is the space between the visceral and parietal layers. Intraperitoneal organs are completely surrounded by this cavity, whereas retroperitoneal organs lie behind (dorsal to) the peritoneum, pressed against the dorsal body wall. The major intraperitoneal organs (and their associated mesenteries) are as follows:

  • Liver – Lesser omentum and falciform ligament
  • Stomach – Greater and lesser omentum
  • Ileum and jejunum – Mesentery proper
  • Transverse colon – Transverse mesocolon
  • Sigmoid colon – Sigmoid mesocolon

Dr. Ahlgren offered a trick for the digestive organs: list the structures in order from the stomach to the rectum, and they alternate between intraperitoneal and retroperitoneal. The one rule is that the jejunum and ileum count as a single unit, while the duodenum stands alone.

  • Stomach – intraperitoneal
  • Duodenum – retroperitoneal
  • Jejunum and ileum – intraperitoneal
  • Ascending colon – retroperitoneal
  • Transverse colon – intraperitoneal
  • Descending colon – retroperitoneal
  • Sigmoid colon – intraperitoneal
  • Rectum – retroperitoneal

(To remember that the ileum is the last part of the small intestine, think of the ileocecal valve, which leads into the cecum.) The pancreas is also retroperitoneal.

The mnemonic "SAD PUCKER" can help you remember the retroperitoneal organs:

  • Suprarenal glands
  • Aorta/IVC
  • Duodenum (2nd-4th parts)
  • Pancreas (except tail)
  • Ureters
  • Colon (ascending & descending)
  • Kidneys
  • Esophagus (lower thoracic part)
  • Rectum (lower 2/3)

Retroperitoneal organs can be classified as either primarily or secondarily retroperitoneal. Primarily retroperitoneal organs, such as the kidneys, develop behind the peritoneal cavity and were never enclosed by it. Secondarily retroperitoneal organs, such as the pancreas, duodenum, and colon, begin development within the peritoneal cavity but become pressed against the dorsal body wall as the gut elongates and folds, eventually losing their mesentery and fusing to the posterior wall. This compression during development explains why certain digestive organs, like parts of the duodenum and colon, are fixed in position rather than freely suspended.

The Mouth, Pharynx, and Esophagus

We will now briefly discuss the anatomy of the alimentary canal organ-by-organ, beginning with the mouth and ending with the rectum.

The oral cavity (or mouth) is a mucosa-lined cavity that consists of thick, nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium. It exists within the boundaries of the labia (lips), cheeks, palate, and tongue.

The tongue is composed primarily of skeletal muscle, which allows it to move voluntarily for actions such as speaking and manipulating food. However, many of its movements during chewing and swallowing occur reflexively, without conscious control. This combination of voluntary and reflexive activity makes the tongue highly coordinated and efficient in directing food through the oral cavity. The tongue has been nicknamed the "Great Dodger" because, despite being positioned between the teeth, it is rarely bitten during normal function.

The basic anatomy of the oral cavity is shown below. Dr. Ahlgren made special note of the following features:

  • The uvula functions much like the epiglottis, swinging back to close off the opening between the oropharynx and the nasopharynx and preventing liquids from entering the nasal cavity. It also plays a role in the gag reflex. The surface of the uvula is covered by nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium, which protects the underlying tissue from irritation caused by foods and liquids, including acidic or abrasive substances that contact the oral cavity.

  • The fauces are the archways that form a narrow passage from the mouth to the pharynx between the base of the tongue and the soft palate. The palatine tonsils are found on the sides of the fauces. Dr. Ahlgren said you do not need to know the names of the individual arches. Think of the fauces as the oral counterpart of the internal nares: the passageway you cross to get from the oral cavity into the oropharynx.

  • The hard palate forms the anterior roof of the mouth and is composed of four bones—the right and left maxillae in front and the right and left palatine bones behind. Running along the midline of the hard palate is the palatine raphe, a line marking the fusion of the palate on the midline. Posterior to the hard palate is the soft palate, a flexible muscular extension that terminates as the uvula. Together, they separate the oral cavity from the nasal cavity and help direct food toward the oropharynx during swallowing.

  • The labial frenulum is a medial fold connecting the lips to the gums. Note that the anatomical term for the gums is gingiva (singular) or gingivae (plural). The teeth are held by two bones, the maxilla and the mandible, and the sockets they sit in are called alveoli.

  • The lingual frenulum is a small fold of tissue that anchors the tongue to the floor of the mouth. If it extends too far toward the tip of the tongue, it can limit the tongue's ability to protrude (stick out)—a condition known as ankyloglossia, or being "tongue-tied." In newborns, this may interfere with nursing, but it can be corrected easily by clipping the frenulum to restore normal tongue movement.

  • The oral vestibule is the space between the teeth and the cheeks or between the teeth and the lips. For example, when you are brushing your teeth, your toothbrush is in the vestibule. (Recall that the nasal cavity also has a vestibule just inside its entrance.)

  • At the base of the lingual frenulum are the openings of the submandibular ducts, which secrete saliva. Laterally to those, you will find the openings of the sublingual ducts. (Because numerous salivary glands lie beneath the tongue, saliva tends to pool under the tongue during dental procedures. This is why dental hygienists often place suction there—to remove the excess saliva that continuously drains from the submandibular and sublingual glands.) The submandibular glands themselves sit near the angle of the mandible and send their ducts forward to this spot. The sublingual glands lie directly beneath each side of the tongue and drain through multiple small ducts.

    Some people can voluntarily force saliva to spray from these ducts in a small stream, a phenomenon known as gleeking. This occurs when the skeletal muscles in the floor of the mouth contract and compress the submandibular ducts, pushing saliva outward—usually triggered by something spicy or sour. (Gleeking serves no purpose, and not everyone can do it.)

  • The parotid gland is the largest salivary gland. It is flat and broad, sits superficially just in front of the ear, and is named for its position beside the ear. The parotid duct is a long duct that crosses the cheek, pierces the buccinator muscle, and empties onto the inner surface of the cheek beside the upper teeth. Anatomy references place the opening next to the upper second molar; Dr. Ahlgren described it as near the upper premolars.

  • The salivary glands are accessory digestive organs. Saliva is a water-based substance, containing enzymes that help us taste and digest food. Additionally, saliva helps protect your teeth from sugar and other substances that may get stuck on the surface of your teeth and cause damage. In a healthy individual, the salivary glands produce approximately 1.5 liters of saliva per day, most of which is swallowed and reabsorbed.

The Tongue

The tongue is the first major muscular organ involved in mechanical digestion. It consists of two kinds of muscles covered by epithelial tissue with specialized cells called taste buds. (It is primarily made of skeletal muscle that produces voluntary movements.)

The tongue is covered with epithelial tissue, which regenerates well. If you drink hot cocoa and burn your tongue, the portion of the epithelial layer that the burn destroyed will soon regenerate.

The surface anatomy of the tongue is best described by dividing the tongue into two parts:

  • The posterior one-third of the tongue – The posterior one-third of the tongue lies outside of the oral cavity, in the oropharynx. The boundary between the oral cavity and the oropharynx is formed by the V-shaped sulcus terminalis (terminal sulcus)—the final groove of the tongue. The bumps on the posterior one-third of the tongue are not taste buds or papillae; they represent the lingual tonsil, a collection of lymphatic tissue. Because the lingual tonsil lies deep in the oropharynx, it is not visible when you look in your mouth under normal conditions. Behind this groove, the tongue turns roughly 90 degrees and runs down the throat, which is another reason this part is hidden from view.

  • The anterior two-thirds of the tongue – The anterior two-thirds of the tongue form the portion of the tongue that lies in the oral cavity proper. This part of the tongue contains visible bumps called papillae. Dr. Ahlgren noted that these visible bumps are not taste buds. Taste buds are microscopic, whereas papillae can be seen with the naked eye. In Dr. Ahlgren's description, a taste bud is a modified epithelial cell wired to a sensory neuron, and where its papilla sits on the tongue determines which cranial nerve carries the signal. Most taste buds sit on the sides of the papillae, down in the creases between them, so food must be broken down small enough, and dissolved well enough in saliva, to slip into those creases and reach them.

Taste information from the tongue is carried to the brain by three cranial nerves. The facial nerve (cranial nerve VII) transmits taste from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, the glossopharyngeal nerve (cranial nerve IX) carries taste from the posterior one-third, and the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) conveys taste sensations from the epiglottis and nearby regions of the throat. Dr. Ahlgren drew the boundary at the terminal sulcus, which puts the vallate papillae (just in front of it) in facial nerve territory by her account. Anatomy references assign the vallate papillae to the glossopharyngeal nerve despite their position.

Dr. Ahlgren emphasized that the classic "tongue map" showing separate regions for sweet, salty, sour, and bitter is incorrect. All areas of the tongue—and even the epiglottis—contain receptors capable of detecting every basic taste. The difference lies in which chemical compounds activate specific receptors, not in where those receptors are located.

The two kinds of muscles in the tongue are as follows:

  • The internal muscles of the tongue are attached to the external muscles but not to bone or cartilage. When they contract, they change the shape of the tongue. When you curl your tongue, you are using the internal muscles.

  • The external muscles of the tongue are attached to the hyoid bone and the bones of the skull. Because one part of the muscle is in a fixed location, contraction of these muscles changes the position of the tongue in the oral cavity.

    One external muscle is the genioglossus muscle, which always has a bit of tone. It qualifies as an external muscle because its front end is anchored to the mandible. From there it fans back through the tongue toward the epiglottis, making it a fairly large muscle, and its steady tone is what holds the tongue forward and out of the airway. Be ready to classify it as internal or external from an image. If you relax this muscle too much during sleep, the tongue will slide back and your airway will become occluded. The body responds by rousing just enough to restore tone and reopen the airway. Dr. Ahlgren noted that people usually do not wake fully; the repeated partial arousals break up their sleep, so they believe they slept through the night when they did not. People who suffer from sleep apnea commonly have this problem.

Pharynx

We discussed the pharynx ("the throat") when we covered the respiratory system. It is also involved in the digestive system, because it moves food from the oral cavity to the esophagus.

Recall that the three sections of the pharynx are the nasopharynx, the oropharynx, and the laryngopharynx. The respiratory system uses all three sections, but the digestive system only uses two: the oropharynx and the laryngopharynx.

Dr. Ahlgren pointed out that the stratified squamous epithelium of the oral cavity and esophagus protects these regions from the rough textures of foods we eat. She gave the example of crunchy, triangle-shaped chips like Doritos that can scrape the mouth or throat, which shows why these areas need a tough, protective lining.

The muscles in the pharynx and neck contract in a specific sequence to complete swallowing. One group lifts the larynx upward to position it beneath the epiglottis and close off the airway. A second group squeezes the pharyngeal walls to push the food into the esophagus, and a third group lowers the larynx again to reopen the airway once the bolus has passed. The first step in swallowing is elevation of the pharynx, which causes the epiglottis to fold down and close off the airway before the bolus is pushed into the esophagus. The epiglottis is elastic cartilage, so it is tempting to assume food simply bends it down. Dr. Ahlgren stressed that the airway is sealed mainly by lifting: the epiglottis tips posteriorly as the structures beneath it are pulled up, and the pharyngeal constrictor muscles then squeeze the bolus past.

The Esophagus

The esophagus is a collapsible muscular tube that extends from the laryngopharynx to the stomach. It runs through the thoracic body cavity and is lined by mucosa and submucosa. Most of the esophagus is covered by an outer adventitia, a fibrous connective tissue layer that anchors it to surrounding structures. Only the short portion within the abdominal cavity has a true serosal covering.

At rest the esophagus is collapsed, and in cross section its lumen is essentially flat and closed. It is also distensible, opening only while something is being swallowed. Its mucosa is therefore folded into rugae: there is more lining than the collapsed tube needs, so the non-stretchy epithelium can unfold rather than tear when the wall expands. Dr. Ahlgren compared this to an elastic waistband, whose fabric bunches when relaxed and lies flat when pulled.

As you move down the esophagus, the muscular portion of the esophageal wall transitions from skeletal muscle to smooth muscle. The top one-third of the esophagus consists primarily of skeletal muscle, which we can consciously control. For example, we use this muscle to swallow food and initiate its propulsion down the GI tract. The middle one-third consists of a mixture of skeletal and smooth muscle. The bottom one-third of the esophagus consists of only smooth muscle, which we cannot voluntarily control.

Because the upper portion of the esophagus contains skeletal muscle, you can sometimes voluntarily move food back into your mouth if it has not traveled too far; however, once it enters the smooth muscle portion, this is no longer possible. Dr. Ahlgren noted that the gag reflex is triggered in the pharynx (especially the oropharynx), not in the esophagus.

The walls of the esophagus consist of nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium, just like the oropharynx and laryngopharynx. There is an abrupt change in the composition of the epithelial tissue when the esophagus dumps into the stomach through the cardiac sphincter. Below the cardiac sphincter, the epithelium becomes simple columnar epithelium rather than stratified squamous epithelium.

In the mediastinum, the esophagus lies directly behind the trachea, against the trachealis muscle that spans the open backs of the tracheal cartilages. Because muscle can stretch, the trachealis gives the esophagus room to bulge forward as a large bolus passes. Dr. Ahlgren added that the uncomfortable "lump" felt when swallowing something too big comes partly from pressure on the trachealis, not only from the esophagus.

The esophagus passes through the diaphragm at an opening called the esophageal hiatus. (The diaphragm also contains two other major openings: the aortic hiatus, which allows the aorta to pass through, and the vena caval hiatus, which allows the inferior vena cava to pass.)

The Stomach

The stomach, which is the widest portion of the alimentary canal, is located in the abdominal cavity. It is the first site of protein breakdown in the alimentary canal. Food sent to the stomach is stored for a period of about four hours. That figure varies with the person and the meal. Chemical digestion technically starts earlier, because saliva begins working on carbohydrates (and arguably some fats) in the mouth and keeps working as food moves down the esophagus, but saliva cannot break down protein. During this time, the stomach churns it so that it makes contact with the lining of the stomach where stomach acid is secreted. Physical and chemical breakdown of the food turns it into a thick, sticky, partly digested paste called chyme.

Gross Anatomy

The stomach is an interesting organ to observe during dissections because you can immediately tell whether the animal or person had eaten a large meal right before dying. If so, the stomach will be distended and puffy. If not, the stomach will be small and shriveled.

The stomach is a J-shaped organ bounded by a smaller concave curve called a lesser curvature and a larger convex curve called a greater curvature. The cardiac region of the stomach is the area near the cardiac sphincter, which controls the flow of materials from the esophagus to the stomach. (This region gets its name because it is located close to the heart.) The fundus is the region superior to the level of the cardiac region; it is a dome-shaped structure that projects upward.

The fundus has functional significance beyond its shape. This region contains a particularly high density of enzyme-producing gastric glands. Because it lies above the rest of the stomach, these secretions can effectively "rain down" onto the food below, ensuring that digestive enzymes mix thoroughly with the stomach contents—similar to how tears from the lacrimal glands wash down over the eye.

As the stomach approaches the duodenum, it funnels down. The narrowing as a whole is the pyloric region, and its final stretch is the pyloric canal. The pyloric sphincter controls the flow of material between the stomach and the duodenum of the small intestine. The pyloric and cardiac sphincters both close when the stomach churns. Both the cardiac and pyloric sphincters are rings of smooth muscle formed where the circular layer of the muscularis externa thickens. The pyloric sphincter is sometimes labeled a valve, but Dr. Ahlgren pointed out that it is a sphincter, not a true valve.

When the cardiac sphincter fails to close tightly, stomach acid can flow backward into the esophagus—a condition known as gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). Because the esophagus lies directly behind the heart, this burning pain is often felt in the chest and referred to as "heartburn." The stomach's simple columnar epithelium can withstand this acid, but the esophageal lining cannot, leading to irritation and discomfort.

Rugae are temporary (i.e., non-permanent) folds in the stomach formed by bunches of mucosal lining. The mucosal lining stretches out when the stomach fills with food, and the rugae disappear. (When we discuss the small intestine, we will mention a similar feature called the plicae circulares. Unlike the rugae, the plicae circulares are permanent structures.) Although rugae are most noticeable in the stomach, Dr. Ahlgren noted that they appear in any distensible portion of the gastrointestinal tract. Because epithelial tissue itself is not stretchy, these folds allow the lining of organs such as the stomach, bladder, and parts of the intestines to expand and then recoil without tearing. Rugae are not arranged the same way throughout the stomach. In the body and most of the pyloric region they are irregular, but in the pyloric canal they straighten into parallel ridges that channel chyme toward the duodenum.

The stomach's walls contain all four layers typically present in the alimentary canal, including the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, and serosa. An interesting difference between the stomach and the rest of the alimentary canal is that its muscularis externa layer has three layers of muscle rather than two. Like the other parts of the alimentary canal, its muscularis externa consists of longitudinal and circular layers of muscle. However, unlike the other parts, it also has an oblique layer of muscle tissue, which forms the deepest layer of the muscularis externa in the stomach. In other words, the extra layer is added on the inside, not the outside: moving from the serosa toward the lumen, the order is longitudinal, circular, oblique. This additional muscle layer optimizes the stomach's ability to mechanically churn things in the stomach. (Dr. Ahlgren compared the churning of the stomach to churning butter — a surprisingly accurate analogy!)

The orientation of the stomach can vary from person to person. In individuals with long torsos, the stomach tends to hang more vertically, while in those with shorter torsos it lies more horizontally, with the lesser curvature situated above the greater curvature. Although this difference has no functional impact, it is a notable anatomical variation.

Tissue Transition at the Cardiac Sphincter

There is a sharp transition in epithelial tissue at the cardiac sphincter. The esophagus is lined with non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, which protects it from abrasion. The stomach, by contrast, is lined with simple columnar epithelium, which is better suited for secretion and absorption. This transition is immediate, unlike the gradual tissue changes found in other parts of the digestive system.

Production of Pepsin

The surface epithelium of the stomach contains gastric pits, which are ducts that lead to gastric glands that produce gastric juices, which help to chemically digest food. These gastric glands are exocrine glands, meaning they secrete their products into ducts that empty into the lumen of the stomach. They protrude deep down into the lamina propria layer of the mucosa. Dr. Ahlgren separated two terms: a gastric pore is the opening on the stomach's surface, while the gastric pit is the wide duct beneath it, which carries secretions but makes none. The secreting part of the gland lies below the pit. The glands are built from the same simple columnar epithelium as the surface, dipping down into tubes, which is why the stomach's mucosa is so thick.

The cells of the gastric glands do not produce pepsin directly. Rather, chief cells deep in the gland produce an inactive precursor called pepsinogen and then transfer it into the duct. When pepsinogen comes in contact with hydrochloric acid (HCl), a strong acid produced by parietal cells, which line the side walls of the gland above the chief cells (pariet- means wall), the two combine to produce pepsin, the active, protein-digesting (proteolytic) form of the enzyme. Pepsin is then secreted into the stomach, where it can assist in the breakdown of proteins.

Note that your esophagus and oral cavity cannot handle highly acidic contents. People with GI disorders or bulimia often bring their stomach contents back up into their esophagi and oral cavities, which damages these regions.

The Small Intestine

The small intestine is the longest part of the alimentary canal. It is the site of most enzymatic digestion and nearly all nutrient absorption. Since the small intestine is so long, it allows us to maximize the time we are able to pull nutrients out of the food we put in our body. Most of the digestive enzymes found in the small intestine are secreted by the pancreas. (Note that the organ is named the small intestine, not the small intestines. The plural form, intestines, refers to the small intestine and the large intestine put together.)

Dr. Ahlgren mentioned that, interestingly, the small intestine appears longer after death because the smooth muscle in its walls loses tone and relaxes, allowing the structure to extend beyond its typical length in a living body.

Recall that the contents of the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract are technically considered part of the external environment, even though they are inside the body. Only after nutrients cross the epithelial lining of the small intestine and enter the bloodstream do they become part of the internal environment. This makes the epithelial layer of the small intestine a critical boundary for absorption.

Chyme moves into the small intestine from the stomach via the pyloric sphincter. It then moves through three segments of the small intestine:

  • The duodenum is the first part of the small intestine, and it is the shortest part. This is a C-shaped retroperitoneal organ. Diagrams make it look as though it swings out beside the stomach, but it actually runs back from the stomach and lies against the posterior body wall. (The jejunum and ileum are intraperitoneal and hang from the mesentery proper, sometimes simply called "the mesentery.")

  • The jejunum is the middle part of the small intestine, which is typically located in the upper-left portion.

  • The ileum is the final, longest part of the small intestine, which is typically located in the lower-right portion and leads into the large intestine. (The ileum is located close to the ilium bone in the pelvis, though the two are spelled differently.)

Contents move through the small intestine via two mechanisms:

  • Segmentation – Whereas the churning of the contents of the stomach occurs in a constant, rhythmic fashion, the movement of the contents of the small intestine occurs in a circular, segmented fashion. This helps with mechanical digestion, and it also propels chyme down the small intestine.

  • Peristalsis – Peristalsis is simply the contraction and relaxation of muscles that propels materials down the small intestine.

The small intestine is the primary site of absorption, so it has a number of modifications that assist in increasing surface area for absorption:

  • Plicae circulares are permanent circular folds in the mucosa that greatly increase surface area for absorption. Their spiral arrangement helps direct chyme distally through the small intestine, but segmentation itself is performed by the muscularis and is not caused by these folds. (In this course the term also appears as plicae circularis.)

    The plicae circulares are kind of like the rugae in the stomach, but they are permanent rather than temporary. No matter how much you stretch the small intestine, these circular folds will not go away.

  • Villi are finger-like projections from the mucosa that are covered with simple columnar epithelium. These projections, which function to increase the surface area for absorption, are about 1 mm tall and can be seen without a microscope. They are fuzzy, bumpy-looking structures. Blood vessels run up into the villi, so nutrients that come into contact with the villi can move across the epithelial wall and diffuse into the capillary beds. Capillary beds and lacteals (structures in the lymph vessel system) run up the middle of these villi. When viewed with the naked eye, the mucosal lining of the small intestine looks like shag carpeting because the villi create a dense, fuzzy texture along the surface.

  • Microvilli are smaller than villi; they are projections on the apical side of each simple columnar epithelial cell on the villi. They function to further increase the surface area for absorption. You cannot see microvilli without a microscope.

Accessory Organs: The Liver, Gallbladder, and Pancreas

Before moving on to the large intestine, we will look at the accessory digestive organs, which are involved in digestion but lie outside of the alimentary canal. In this section, we will discuss three accessory organs: the liver, gallbladder, and pancreas.

The Liver and Gallbladder

The liver is the largest gland in the body. It produces bile, a green liquid that plays a role in digestion by emulsifying fats. Bile produced by the liver is stored in the gallbladder, a muscular sac found in the underside of the liver's right lobe. The inner lining of the gallbladder contains rugae, which allow the sac to expand as it fills with stored bile. The gallbladder has a green appearance because it stores green-colored bile.

Because the liver is a gland, it is composed primarily of hepatocytes (epithelial-derived cells) that regenerate rapidly; this is why the liver heals exceptionally well.

The liver has four lobes: two giant primary lobes (left and right) and two smaller lobes (the quadrate lobe and the caudate lobe). Two ligaments found in the liver include the ligamentum teres hepatis (which divides the left part of the liver into medial and lateral sections) and the falciform ligament (which attaches the liver to the anterior body wall).

Bile is produced in the left and right lobes of the liver. It drains into the left and right hepatic ducts, then into the common hepatic duct. The common hepatic duct joins with the cystic duct to make the common bile duct, which dumps into the duodenum. The major duodenal papilla forms a small, visible bump on the inner wall of the duodenum where the common bile duct and pancreatic duct open into the small intestine.

The common bile duct and the pancreatic duct join together before entering the duodenum at a shared opening called the major duodenal papilla. The flow of both bile and pancreatic secretions through this opening is regulated by a sphincter, which controls when these substances are released into the small intestine.

The distal sphincters of the bile duct are closed when the small intestine no longer needs bile. When this occurs, bile from the common bile duct backs up into the cystic duct and moves into the gallbladder for storage. Bile cannot flow back into the liver because a small unidirectional valve prevents its return, so when the distal sphincter is closed, the accumulating bile naturally moves into the gallbladder instead.

People sometimes have to have their gallbladders removed because crystallized salts form gallstones that can block the cystic duct, causing pain—especially when the gallbladder contracts against the obstruction during a fatty meal. Although this condition can be very painful, it is not typically life-threatening. In contrast, a gallstone that obstructs the major duodenal papilla is much more dangerous because it can block the release of pancreatic enzymes, which are essential for digestion and nutrient absorption. People whose gallbladders have been removed are less efficient in digesting fat. (During gallbladder removal, surgeons typically leave part of the cystic duct intact, and this remaining segment can function like a small "mini gallbladder" that provides limited bile storage.)

The walls of the bile ducts also contain smooth muscle, which helps propel bile in the correct direction when the gallbladder contracts.

When chyme enters the duodenum and the small intestine needs bile, bile is released from the gallbladder through the cystic duct. The cystic duct then empties into the bile duct and into the duodenum.

Dr. Ahlgren mentioned that primary biliary cholangitis is an autoimmune condition in which the body progressively destroys the bile ducts within the liver. This condition can develop slowly over many years, eventually causing bile to accumulate in the liver and leading to liver damage and cirrhosis. This example highlights the importance of intact bile ducts for normal digestion and liver function.

The Pancreas

The pancreas is a complex organ with a relatively simple anatomy. It is a gland with both exocrine and endocrine functions. Its exocrine function is to produce the majority of the enzymes used for chemical digestion. Its endocrine function is to secrete hormones that regulate blood glucose levels, including insulin and glucagon.

The pancreas has three sections: a large head, an elongated body, and a pointed tail. The head looks like it "snuggles up" to the C-shaped curve of the duodenum, and the tail rests against the spleen.

The pancreatic duct runs through the middle of the pancreas. It receives secretions from other smaller fractional ducts running throughout the pancreas, and it carries those secretions to the major duodenal papilla, which is the interface between the pancreas and the duodenum. The major duodenal papilla acts as a sphincter to regulate the secretions from both the pancreatic duct and the common bile duct.

Note that there is also a smaller accessory duct either above or below the pancreatic duct that allows some enzymes and other secretions to get into the duodenum via an alternative route. This arrangement is an example of functional redundancy, meaning the pancreas has a backup pathway to deliver digestive enzymes to the small intestine if the primary route (through the major duodenal papilla) becomes obstructed, such as by a gallstone.

Pancreatic enzymes play a crucial role in digestion and nutrition. People with pancreatic cancer often become extremely emaciated very quickly. This is because their pancreases no longer produce enough enzymes to properly digest their food, and they suffer from malnutrition.

The Large Intestine

The large intestine (commonly called the colon, although strictly the colon is only part of the large intestine; the two terms are used interchangeably in everyday speech) receives anything that is not absorbed in the small intestine. Its major function is to reabsorb water and electrolytes from this material. As chyme enters the large intestine and enters the cecum through the ileocecal valve, it is relatively liquefied. By the time feces pass out of the GI tract, it is much more solid because water has been reabsorbed. If we did not reabsorb water in the large intestine, we would be in a constant state of diarrhea and dehydration.

Chyme leaves the stomach thick and pasty. In the small intestine it thins out, because enzymes and bile arrive suspended in water-based fluid, and it keeps getting more watery along the way, so what reaches the large intestine is essentially liquid. Much of the water found in the material entering the cecum comes from the watery matrix that carries digestive enzymes into the small intestine, so the large intestine must reabsorb this water and the dissolved electrolytes before waste is eliminated. When the large intestine cannot reabsorb this water—such as during diarrhea—the body loses significant fluid that would normally return to the bloodstream. This reduces the amount of water in the blood and impairs the delivery of nutrients and other substances throughout the body.

Peristalsis constantly moves materials along the stomach and small intestine. Constant peristalsis does not occur in the large intestine. Rather, the large intestine engages in mass peristaltic movement, which infrequently purges materials that have been sitting in the large intestine for quite a while. If the constant peristalsis occurred in the large intestine, you would be pooping all the time!

Mass peristaltic movement is triggered when the cecum becomes distended with material. This stretch activates the nervous system, which initiates a coordinated wave of peristalsis to move contents through the large intestine and toward the rectum.

Gross Anatomy of the Large Intestine

The diagram below labels the major portions of the gross anatomy of the colon:

Chyme from the ileum of the small intestine enters the cecum of the large intestine through the ileocecal valve. (You might describe the ileocecal valve as a "sphincterish type valve" because it is stretch-dependent. As the cecum fills up, it closes.) As the cecum fills and expands, the increasing distension physically compresses and narrows the ileocecal valve opening, causing it to close and preventing additional material from entering. The vermiform appendix is suspended by a little bit of mesentery and attached to the cecum.

From the cecum the contents go up the ascending colon. They then turn a corner called the right colic flexure. (This is also called the right hepatic flexure because it is located next to the liver.) From the right colic flexure, contents go through the transverse colon. They then turn another corner at the left colic flexure. (This is also called the left splenic flexure because it is located next to the spleen.) Contents then go down the descending colon, to the sigmoid colon, which twists twice. (The sigmoid colon forms an S-shaped curve that lies horizontally rather than vertically.) The contents then enter the rectum, which funnels into an anal canal. The anal canal ends at the anus, which is controlled by sphincters called the anal sphincters.

The large intestine has several specialized features:

  • Teniae coli are thickenings of the longitudinal layer of the muscularis externa that form three large stripes along the outside of the colon. When they contract, they shrink the length of the large intestine. They form haustra, which are discussed below.

  • A haustrum is a bulge formed by the tenseness of the teniae coli. (Haustrum is the singular form of haustra.) These bulges make the large intestine look bumpy.

  • Epiploic appendages are pouches of visceral peritoneum filled with fat. These structures hang from the intestines of humans and animals, but they are not found anywhere else in the body. Although they are visible and prominent, we don't know much about their significance or function.

The large intestine also has many goblet cells, which produce mucus that lubricates the passage of waste. As material moves distally and becomes more solid due to water reabsorption, the number of goblet cells increases. This additional mucus reduces friction and protects the intestinal wall from tearing, which is especially important in the distal large intestine because a rupture here could expose blood vessels to fecal material.

Intense endurance exercise can sometimes trigger an urgent need to defecate. This occurs because prolonged running increases intestinal motility and decreases the time available for water reabsorption in the large intestine, which may lead to the sudden passage of loose stool in some individuals.

The Rectum, Anus, and Anal Sphincters

The rectum is a retroperitoneal organ because it does not contain the extra layer of mesentery. As you move distally, the rectum narrows down and becomes the anal canal. There are ridges that have formed at the last part of the anal canal and right below these ridges is a very distinct line. This line is called the pectinate line. Inside the distal rectum, the mucosal lining forms longitudinal ridges called anal columns that channel material downward toward the anal canal, functioning as directional structures that guide waste toward the exit.

The rectal valves are internal folds within the rectum that help regulate the passage of fecal matter and gas. These transverse folds are made of both mucosal and muscular layers and play a key role in maintaining continence. By creating partial barriers within the rectum, they allow gas to pass through while holding back solid waste, enabling the body to distinguish between the two. This selective passage is what gives healthy individuals the ability to pass gas without unintentionally releasing stool. While not often discussed, these structures are important for everyday digestive function and help maintain control over bowel movements.

The anus is a transition from the mucosal lining of the rectum to the skin outside the body. That is, it is a transition between a mucous epithelial membrane and a cutaneous epithelial membrane. This is where the simple columnar epithelium lining transitions to a keratinized, stratified squamous epithelium.

A set of two sphincters control the movement of materials out of the anal canal:

  • The internal anal sphincter is a thickening of the circularly arranged portion of the muscularis externa. It consists of smooth muscle, so we have no volitional control over it.
  • The external anal sphincter consists of skeletal muscle that we can control.

Another muscle involved in the control of voluntary defecation is the levator ani muscle, which lifts the anus. The muscle contracts and shortens, lifting the whole anus up and out so that defecation occurs more smoothly.

Normal adults can obviously control when and where they defecate. When the rectum fills with feces, stretch (or distention) receptors send signals to neurons in the spinal cord, triggering a spinal reflex that causes contraction of the walls of the rectum and relaxation of the internal anal sphincter. This tells you that you have to defecate. When you find a time to do so, you voluntarily inhibit the motor neurons that innervate the external anal sphincter, and you allow feces to pass out of the rectum and (hopefully) into the toilet.

Clinical Correlation: Hemorrhoids

A hemorrhoid is a swollen vein within the mucosal lining or submucosa of the anus. These veins become inflamed because they are varicose veins, which have weakened walls and valves. Two types of hemorrhoid are as follows:

  • External hemorrhoids are inferior to the pectinate line. These hemorrhoids are very painful but don't tend to bleed much at all.
  • Internal hemorrhoids are superior to the pectinate line. Typically, these hemorrhoids are not felt as much but they bleed a lot.

A common treatment for symptomatic internal hemorrhoids is rubber-band ligation, in which a small band is placed at the base of the swollen vein to cut off its blood supply so it shrivels and falls off.

Note that the color of blood in stool can help indicate the location of bleeding within the gastrointestinal tract. Bright red blood typically indicates bleeding from the distal portion of the large intestine or rectum, such as from hemorrhoids, because the blood has not been digested. In contrast, black or tarry stool suggests bleeding from higher up in the gastrointestinal tract, where the blood has been digested as it moves through the system.
