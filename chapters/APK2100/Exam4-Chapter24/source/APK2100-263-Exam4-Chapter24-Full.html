Introduction to the Urinary System

The urinary system is responsible for eliminating wastes from the body, as well as regulating the chemical constancy and volume of blood. The urinary system maintains an appropriate balance of water, acids, electrolytes, and bases. It also plays an important role in regulating blood pressure. Because it regulates the chemical constancy of the blood, the urinary system also regulates the chemical constancy of the body's fluids.

The most important organs of the urinary system are the kidneys. You have two kidneys, one on your right side and one on your left. The right kidney sits lower than the left kidney in the body because the liver is so large that it pushes the kidney down a bit. (The adrenal glands are located on top of the kidneys, but they are not part of the urinary system.) The kidneys are located in the superior lumbar region of the posterior abdominal wall, deep to the false ribs. Each kidney lies deep to ribs 11 and 12, which provide only partial bony protection. Because these are floating ribs rather than ribs connected to the sternum, the kidneys are more exposed than organs fully enclosed by the ribcage.

Extending down medially from the kidney are the ureters, muscular tube-like structures that act to funnel urine into the urinary bladder. We call this the urinary bladder to distinguish it from the other bladder-like structures of the body. Urine is stored in the urinary bladder until it is sent out of the body through the urethra.

As the ureters descend from the kidneys, they pass in front of the psoas major muscle and then cross anterior to the external iliac artery and vein on their way to the urinary bladder. In cadaveric views, this same pathway is visible as the ureter travels across the surface of the psoas and approaches the bifurcation of the common iliac artery into its internal and external branches. In many dissections, the urinary bladder is cut open to show its interior, so the urethra may not be visible.

While urine formation is an important function of the kidneys, it is only part of their role. When urine is produced, the water used to make that urine comes directly from the blood plasma. Because of this, the kidneys are constantly regulating solute concentrations and helping maintain a stable blood volume as they filter and adjust the chemical makeup of the blood.

How Well Protected Is the Kidney?

Because the kidneys are retroperitoneal, they are not surrounded by the peritoneal cavity or supported by mesenteries. As a result, they lack the additional cushioning and stabilization provided by these structures and lie directly against the posterior abdominal wall, making them more vulnerable to injury than organs that are fully enclosed within the peritoneal cavity.

Just as you can live with one lung missing but not two, you can also live without one kidney but you cannot live without two. Even though the kidneys are vital organs, they are not as well protected as some of the other organs. For example, the brain is protected by a bony case called a skull and the lungs and heart are protected by the ribcage, sternum, vertebrae, and thick musculature. In contrast, the kidneys are protected by a thinner musculature and bones that aren't even directly connected to the sternum.

A 'kidney punch' in boxing can be dangerous, because it can bruise the kidneys. In rougher contact sports, such as football, a strong blow can still bruise the kidneys or cause significant pain, but the most severe injuries—such as a torn vascular attachment—are typically associated with major trauma like a car accident or a fall onto a hard or sharp surface. For this reason, kidney punches are illegal moves in boxing.

Dr. Ahlgren noted that she and her husband both train in martial arts and often watch UFC fights, and they cringe whenever a strike comes close to the kidney region because they know how dangerous kidney trauma can be. Even a glancing blow in this area can cause significant pain, and a harder strike can lead to more serious injury.

Kidney trauma can range from a simple surface bruise to a deeper laceration or, in the most severe cases, a torn vascular attachment. A bruise may occur from a direct blow to the back, while a true laceration requires a much stronger impact. A torn attachment—where the vessels supplying the kidney are disrupted—occurs only in major trauma, such as a car accident or a high fall.

Together, the kidneys receive roughly 20% of the cardiac output at rest, which means a large volume of blood is flowing through these organs at all times. This high blood flow is why a deep laceration or a torn vascular attachment can lead to rapid internal bleeding.

The kidneys do have some additional protections to compensate for the relative lack of protection by bones or a thick musculature. For example, the renal capsule (also called the fibrous capsule) is a thick capsule that wraps its way around the kidneys. The kidney is also protected by a layer of perirenal fat. There are also anterior and posterior bands of renal fascia around the kidney. These bands help keep the kidney in place and protect it from damage.

Gross Anatomy of the Kidney

Superficially, the kidneys actually do look like kidney beans! A frontal section of the kidney reveals that it has a light, consistently colored outer portion called a renal cortex and a striped inner portion called a renal medulla. The cortex is anchored into the renal medulla by extensions called renal columns.

The dark, triangular areas in the medullary portion of the diagram above are renal pyramids. Although they appear as triangles in this cross-section, they actually form pyramidal shapes in the kidney. (You can use the 3D models in lab to get a better idea of what they look like.) The renal medulla consists of both the renal pyramids and columns.

The pointy, nipple-like ends of each renal pyramid are called renal papillae. Each individual papilla dumps into a small collecting tube called a minor calyx. Multiple minor calyces combine to form a major calyx. The minor and major calyces dump into the renal pelvis, which is like the top part of a funnel. The renal pelvis then narrows down and becomes the ureter, which is like the narrow, tubular portion of a funnel.

The renal pelvis, renal major calyces, blood vessels, nerves, and fat exist inside a cavity in the kidney called the renal sinus. The perirenal fat completely fills the gaps within the renal sinus on the medial surface, surrounding and supporting the vessels and collecting structures in that space. Blood vessels and nerves enter the kidney through the renal hilus (also called the renal hilum), which is similar to the hilum of the lung.

Students often confuse the terms renal pelvis, renal sinus, and renal hilum, so be sure to understand the difference between them. The renal sinus is the internal cavity within the kidney that contains fat, vessels, nerves, and the renal pelvis. The renal pelvis is the funnel-shaped collecting region that receives urine from the major calyces. The renal hilum (or hilus) is the external indentation where vessels, nerves, and the ureter enter or exit the kidney. These are three distinct structures and are not interchangeable.

Microscopic Anatomy of the Kidney

The nephron is the smallest functional unit of the kidney. It consists of two parts: the renal corpuscle and a tubular section.

  • Renal corpuscle – The renal corpuscle exists in the cortex only; it does not extend into the medulla. It consists of a tuft of fenestrated capillaries called the glomerulus, which is surrounded by a hollow, cup-shaped capsule called the glomerular capsule (or Bowman's capsule). The capillaries in the glomerulus are arranged in a strange, tangled way, similar to the choroid plexus in the brain.

    • When you look at the Bowman's capsule, you can see that the outside is made up of simple squamous epithelium. As the Bowman's capsule approaches the base of the glomerulus, the simple squamous epithelium starts folding in on top of the glomerular capillaries. These epithelial cells then change to a very specialized type of epithelial cells called the podocytes. These podocytes are only found at the glomerulus. Podocytes form lots of branches that interdigitate. These branches of the podocytes act as a barrier for the capillaries, so that fluid cannot just rush out of them. The interlocking podocyte processes act like a strainer. Filtered material escapes through the fenestrations and intercellular clefts of the capillary wall and then through the slits between podocyte processes. Glomerular capillaries are fenestrated because clefts alone would not let material out quickly enough.

    The filtration membrane consists of all the layers fluid must pass through. This multilayered filtration membrane is structurally similar to the respiratory membrane in the lungs, where substances must pass through both a capillary wall and an epithelial layer before entering a separate space.

    Since the glomerular capsule has two layers, the outer simple squamous epithelium is the parietal layer and the podocytes form the visceral layer. This two-layered arrangement with a space in between resembles a serous membrane: the visceral layer is the one in contact with the glomerular capillaries, and the parietal layer is the one that is not. Both the capillary wall and the capsule are simple squamous epithelium, joined by a very thin layer of connective tissue. During filtration, material leaves the blood through the capillary wall, crosses the visceral layer, and lands in the capsular space. The capsular space is continuous with the proximal convoluted tubule, so anything that enters it is technically in the external environment. Whatever stays in the tubule lumen becomes urine; whatever is pulled back across the tubule wall into the peritubular capillaries is returned to the body.

  • Tubular section – The tubular section consists of a series of tubes with distinct names, including the proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, and collecting duct.

Introduction to the Urinary System (Continued)

All nephrons have renal corpuscles in the cortex area. However, some nephrons (called juxtamedullary nephrons) have loops of Henle that extend deep into the medulla, while others (called cortical nephrons) have loops of Henle that end near the junction of the cortex and the medulla. About 90-95% of all nephrons in the kidney are cortical nephrons, but we will be focusing on the structures of juxtamedullary nephrons because they play a more important role in the production of urine. The renal corpuscle of a cortical nephron sits high in the cortex, while the renal corpuscle of a juxtamedullary nephron sits lower in the cortex, closer to the medulla. In both cases the corpuscle itself stays in the cortex; only the loop of Henle dips into the medulla.

Blood enters the glomerulus through the afferent arteriole, and it exits the glomerulus through the efferent arteriole. Note that the blood is still oxygenated when it leaves the glomerulus; the glomerulus is simply where the blood gets filtered. Because no gas exchange occurs in the glomerulus, the blood leaving it is still arterial, which is why it exits through an efferent arteriole rather than a vein. The efferent arteriole also contains a greater amount of smooth muscle than a venous structure, allowing the kidney to regulate its diameter and control blood flow into the peritubular capillaries. The afferent arteriole is usually slightly wider than the efferent arteriole, which can help you tell the two apart in a diagram.

From the glomerulus, the filtrate enters a long system of tubes lined with thin epithelium. First, the filtrate enters the proximal convoluted tubule, then proceeds down the descending limb of the loop of Henle, then back up the ascending limb, then into the distal convoluted tubule, and finally to the collecting duct. The collecting duct extends deep down into the medulla. As it approaches the renal papilla, it is specifically referred to as the papillary duct. Multiple papillary ducts open at the tip of the renal papilla and drain urine into a minor calyx. Once fluid leaves the papillary duct and enters a minor calyx, it is considered final urine and can no longer be modified. Urine then flows from the minor calyces into the major calyces, into the renal pelvis, and then into the ureter, to the bladder, and out the urethra.

The tubular section of the nephron is surrounded by blood vessels. The peritubular capillaries extend from the efferent arteriole and cling to the proximal and distal convoluted tubules in the renal cortex. They eventually empty into veins. The vasa recta are specialized, highly ordered capillaries that are associated with the loops of Henle of juxtamedullary nephrons. (The vasa recta are still a type of peritubular capillaries, they are just given a more specific name because they have a structural difference when compared to normal peritubular capillaries.)

EXAM TIP: Vasa Recta Association

Remember that the vasa recta are only associated with the loops of Henle of juxtamedullary nephrons.

The kidney's blood supply includes two capillary beds in sequence: the glomerular capillaries followed by the peritubular capillaries (or the vasa recta in juxtamedullary nephrons). Dr. Ahlgren compared this arrangement to the hepatic portal system, where one capillary bed drains into another rather than directly into a vein. Two capillary beds arranged in series like this are called a portal system. This comparison is meant to highlight the unusual "capillary-bed-to-capillary-bed" organization in the kidney.

The peritubular capillaries associated with cortical nephrons form a typical, highly convoluted capillary bed with no organized pattern. In contrast, the vasa recta surrounding the loops of Henle in juxtamedullary nephrons are long, straight, highly ordered vessels that run parallel to the nephron. This structural difference is the reason the vasa recta are given a distinct name, even though they are still considered a type of peritubular capillary.

Proximal convoluted tubule cells are equipped with microvilli to increase surface area for reabsorption. Dr. Ahlgren highlighted a higher number of mitochondria in these cells as they gather nutrients to send back to the body. Distal convoluted tubule cells have very few if any microvilli and lower number of mitochondria. However, they do have a lot of receptors that receive signals that regulate the movement of substances.

Movement of substances in the distal convoluted tubule is highly regulated because these cells contain numerous receptors on their plasma membranes. These receptors allow the DCT to respond to signals from the blood and adjust ion and water movement as needed, unlike the largely unregulated reabsorption that occurs in the proximal convoluted tubule.

The Juxtaglomerular Apparatus

The juxtaglomerular apparatus (also called the juxtaglomerular complex) is a structure that helps the kidneys regulate blood pressure. The kidneys help regulate blood pressure by adjusting blood flow through the glomerulus and by releasing signaling molecules that influence blood pressure. Changes in filtrate flow through the distal tubule are detected by the macula densa cells, which signal the granular cells to adjust blood flow or release hormones that increase or decrease blood pressure as needed.

The juxtaglomerular apparatus is a point of contact between the afferent arteriole and the distal convoluted tubule, which loops back and passes directly between the afferent and efferent arterioles. This region contains the macula densa cells of the distal tubule and the granular cells of the arteriolar wall, which communicate to help regulate blood flow into the glomerulus. Diagrams often stretch the nephron out from left to right, but that is not anatomically accurate. In reality the tubule folds back so that the distal tubule passes directly between the afferent and efferent arterioles of its own renal corpuscle.

The juxtaglomerular apparatus has specialized cells that control blood throughput (and thus the amount of urine that's produced, blood volume, and blood pressure):

  • Macula densa cells are modified epithelial cells inside the wall of the distal tubule. The wall of the renal tubule is simple cuboidal epithelium, and the macula densa cells are specialized cells within that wall. These cells sense the solutes, especially sodium and chloride, passing over them and signal the granular cells when adjustments to blood flow or pressure are needed. A large amount of sodium chloride means a lot of filtration has occurred, so the macula densa releases a chemical messenger that makes the afferent arteriole constrict. Less blood reaches the glomerulus, less is filtered, less sodium chloride reaches the macula densa, and the signal stops. Because solute load reflects blood volume, this is a built-in way for the kidney to regulate blood volume and therefore blood pressure.
  • Granular cells are modified smooth muscle cells in the afferent arteriole that secrete hormones that cause the afferent arteriole to either dilate or constrict. (Although granular cells are most abundant in the afferent arteriole, a smaller number may also be present in the efferent arteriole, but not to the same extent.)

These cells regulate the amount of blood that gets filtered by the glomerulus. When the glomerulus filters more blood, the kidneys produce more urine. Urine is mostly water, so when the kidneys produce more urine, the blood volume falls. When the blood volume falls, blood pressure falls as well.

Basic Processes of Urine Production

Urine production can be understood as a three-step process that occurs in a specific sequence: filtration, reabsorption, and secretion.

  • Filtration – Blood is filtered in the renal corpuscle. The force of blood pressure pushes plasma (water and dissolved substances) out through the fenestrated capillaries of the glomerulus and into Bowman's capsule. Filtration is a relatively non-specific process, and only part of the plasma is filtered; blood cells and proteins do not cross the filtration membrane. Only about 20% of what enters the glomerulus is filtered; the remaining 80% or so leaves through the efferent arteriole. The blood that is not filtered exits the renal corpuscle through the efferent arteriole.
  • Reabsorption – Because filtration is not selective, some valuable materials (such as water) end up in the filtrate. The peritubular capillaries selectively reabsorb these materials and return them to the bloodstream.
  • Secretion – Some substances in the blood that were not filtered may be selectively secreted from the peritubular capillaries into the tubular portion of the nephron.

As you can see, the peritubular capillaries cooperate with the tubular portion of the nephron in the processes of reabsorption and secretion to determine the final volume and composition of the urine.

The Ureters

Urine drains from the kidneys into the ureters, which extend from the renal pelvis in the medial aspect of the kidney to the urinary bladder. (The ureters begin around the L2 vertebrae.) As the ureters descend toward the urinary bladder, each one passes directly over the sacroiliac (SI) joint region. This relationship can be seen when tracing the ureters from the level of L2 down toward the pelvis.

There is smooth muscle present in the walls of the ureters to move the urine to the bladder. When urine first enters a ureter, the fluid stretches the ureteral wall. This stretch activates peristaltic waves in the smooth muscle, which actively push the urine down the ureter toward the bladder. (At first, you might find this unnecessary because the kidneys are placed high so it makes sense for the urine to just drain down the ureters. The smooth muscle is necessary because of where the ureters drain into the urinary bladder.) The smooth muscle of the ureter is arranged circularly, which lets it act as a pump. When the bladder is empty, gravity alone could deliver urine. Because the ureters open at the base of the bladder, however, urine arriving later must be pushed in against the pressure of the urine already there, so the ureter has to generate a higher pressure than the bladder.

Urine enters the urinary bladder through the ureters at an angle on the posterior-inferior wall. As the bladder fills, the increasing internal pressure compresses these ureteral openings, which helps prevent urine from flowing backward into the ureters. This mechanism is important for preventing reflux of urine toward the kidneys.

Note that, even though the pictures in your textbook show the ureters draining into the top of the urinary bladder, they actually drain into the bottom, posterior portion of the urinary bladder. (This is a common mistake, even in pictures found in anatomy textbooks.) The ureters are usually about 10 inches long, though their length varies by body size.

Kidney stones sometimes form from salts and extra materials in the urinary system. They may vary in size and shape, but they are often jagged, which causes passing them to be painful. If the stones are too large to be passed through the ureters or urethra, they may be surgically removed or medically reduced.

Passing a kidney stone can be extremely painful. Some stones can also be surprisingly large, which may require medical procedures to break them into smaller pieces before they can pass through the ureter or urethra. Stones are made of different substances, so they vary in appearance. A stone hurts in the ureter because the muscular wall contracts down on a hard, jagged object. Once it drops into the bladder it is usually not felt, because the lumen is so large. It becomes painful again in the urethra, not because the urethra squeezes it but because the stone scrapes a narrow, delicate tube on the way out. Stones can be broken into pieces to be passed, or retrieved directly.

In class, Dr. Ahlgren described a case involving her father, who developed very large kidney stones that became lodged in the renal pelvis and could not be removed surgically. Although urine was still able to pass around the stones, they acted as a site for recurrent bacterial infections, which repeatedly led to kidney failure. This example highlights how kidney stones can pose serious risks beyond pain by increasing the likelihood of infection.

The Urinary Bladder

Urine drains from the ureters into the urinary bladder via the ureteric orifices. The urinary bladder is a hollow organ that consists primarily of smooth muscle. Its major function is to store urine and expel it when appropriate. The detrusor muscle is the smooth muscle along the outer wall of the urinary bladder.

Because the urinary bladder is made of smooth muscle, the detrusor muscle itself is not under voluntary control. However, urination can be voluntarily regulated through the external urethral sphincter, which is made of skeletal muscle. This allows you to consciously delay or initiate urination, even though the contraction of the bladder itself is controlled by the autonomic nervous system.

The urinary bladder lies directly behind the pubic symphysis and inferior to the peritoneal cavity. The bladder sits inferior to the peritoneal cavity because the peritoneum actually drapes over the top of it rather than enclosing it. This creates a space where the bladder lies below the peritoneal lining even though it is not considered retroperitoneal. The female bladder tends to be somewhat smaller than the male bladder, and the uterus rests directly on top of it.

If your bladder is very full and you really have to go, the urinary bladder can even be palpated just above the pubic symphysis. ("Holding it" for so long is dangerous, however, so you should urinate immediately at this point.)

When studying pelvic models, it is important to 'sex the model' before identifying urinary or reproductive structures. Dr. Ahlgren emphasized that the male urinary bladder is often mistaken for a uterus on lab models, so always determine whether the image or model shows male or female anatomy before labeling structures. A quick way to do this on a lab model is to check for a penis: if one is present, the hollow muscular organ behind the pubic symphysis is the bladder, not a uterus.

The Urethra

Urine from the urinary bladder drains into the urethra, which will carry it out of the body.

Recall that defecation involved two sets of sphincters, one of which was voluntary and consisted of skeletal muscle and one of which was involuntary and consisted of smooth muscle. The same is true with micturition, or urination. The two sets of sphincters that control urination are as follows:

  • The internal urethral sphincter is created by a thickening of the smooth muscle at the base of the bladder wall. This ring of smooth muscle surrounds the proximal urethra and is under autonomic control. During urination, parasympathetic activity causes this sphincter to relax, allowing urine to pass into the urethra.

  • The external urethral sphincter consists of voluntary skeletal muscle and is formed within the urogenital diaphragm. The urogenital diaphragm is part of the broader pelvic floor—a bowl-shaped sheet of skeletal muscle forming the floor of the pelvic cavity—and it contains openings that allow the urethra (and, in females, the vagina) to pass through it. Circular skeletal muscle fibers in this region create the external urethral sphincter.

When you're "holding your pee," you are contracting your external urethral sphincter. This is the only urethral sphincter under your voluntary control.

There are gender-based differences in urethral structure:

  • Males – Males have a relatively long urethra with three regions: the intermediate-length prostatic region (which passes through the prostate gland), the very short membranous region (which passes through the urogenital diaphragm), and the very long spongy region (which passes through the spongy tissue of the penis). The spongy urethra runs through the corpus spongiosum and ends at the external urethral orifice, the opening to the outside. Both sexes have this opening, and it is where a catheter is inserted.

    Both males and females have a smooth triangular region called the trigone, which is formed by the ureteral orifices and the internal urethral orifice. Because of its location between the openings of the ureters and the urethra, the trigone is a common site for infections. Pathogens that enter through the urethra can quickly reach this region and potentially spread toward the ureters and kidneys.

    The internal walls of male and female bladders also contain rugae, or temporary folds that stretch out and disappear when the bladder fills up. (These are similar to the temporary folds in the stomach, which are called rugae as well.)

    One difference between the internal and external anal sphincters and the internal and external urethral sphincters is their proximity to one another in males. Whereas the internal and external anal sphincters are located right next to one another, the internal and external urethral sphincters are separated by the prostatic urethra.

  • Females – The female urethra is much shorter than the male urethra. It is not divided into regions, and the internal and external urethral sphincter are located much closer to one another than in males, but still separated. Urinary tract infections are more common in females than in males because the urethra is shorter and located near the anal region.

The pelvic floor works together with the diaphragm to regulate pressure within the abdominopelvic cavity. At rest, the diaphragm is dome-shaped and the pelvic floor forms a supportive sling at the bottom of the cavity. When the diaphragm contracts and moves downward, the pelvic floor muscles contract and lift upward, creating opposing forces that increase internal pressure and stabilize the core. This increased pressure helps support the spine and is especially important during activities such as lifting, carrying heavy objects, and maintaining posture. Because of this functional relationship, exercises that target both the pelvic floor (such as Kegels) and surrounding musculature (such as squats) can help improve stability and support. In a superior view of the female pelvic floor, the openings from posterior to anterior are the anus (just in front of the tip of the coccyx), the vaginal orifice, and the urethral orifice. A male pelvic floor lacks the vaginal orifice. Because these are skeletal muscles, they can be contracted voluntarily and strengthened. A weak pelvic floor can cause urine leakage, for example when running, coughing, laughing, or sneezing, and childbirth can stretch and damage these muscles.

The Process of Micturition

Like defecation, the process of micturition (or urination) begins with a stretch signal. When the detrusor muscle stretches out, stretch-sensitive sensory neurons send information into the central nervous system, which is relayed to the pontine micturition center in the pons. This center integrates the sensory input with signals from higher brain centers to determine whether urination should occur.

The pontine micturition center coordinates this process by adjusting autonomic and somatic output. During urination, it inhibits sympathetic activity (which normally keeps the bladder relaxed) and inhibits somatic motor neurons to the external urethral sphincter, allowing it to relax. At the same time, it activates parasympathetic motor neurons, which contract the detrusor muscle and promote bladder emptying.

Urination requires three things:

  • Opening the external urethral sphincter
  • Opening the internal urethral sphincter
  • Squeezing the walls of the bladder to force urine out

At rest, sympathetic motor neurons keep the detrusor muscle relaxed and help keep the internal urethral sphincter closed. During urination, sympathetic activity decreases while parasympathetic motor neurons become active. This parasympathetic input contracts the detrusor muscle and relaxes the internal urethral sphincter. At the same time, somatic motor neurons to the external urethral sphincter are inhibited, allowing it to relax voluntarily. This inhibition happens in the spinal cord: the somatic motor neurons stop releasing acetylcholine onto the sphincter's skeletal muscle. Bladder contraction is parasympathetic because urination is a "rest and digest" function.

Clinical Example: Polycystic Kidney Disease

Polycystic kidney disease is a genetic condition in which numerous fluid-filled cysts develop throughout the kidneys, causing them to become greatly enlarged and impairing their function. Dr. Ahlgren showed images of kidneys affected by this condition, which appeared dramatically enlarged and covered in cysts, highlighting the severe structural changes that can occur. In the case Dr. Ahlgren described, each diseased kidney weighed roughly 11 pounds. The donor kidney was implanted first, and the diseased kidneys were removed about six months later, once the transplant was functioning.

In severe cases, individuals may require dialysis to perform the filtering functions of the kidneys and may eventually need a kidney transplant. This condition illustrates how structural changes in the kidney can significantly disrupt its ability to regulate fluid balance and remove waste.
