Introduction to the Digestive System

The digestive system consists of the alimentary canal and accessory digestive organs. It plays the following roles in the body:

  • Ingestion – Taking in food through the mouth.

  • Propulsion – Material must keep moving along the tube. Propulsion can be voluntary (swallowing) or involuntary; the involuntary form is called peristalsis. The upper (proximal) esophagus is under voluntary control because its muscle is skeletal rather than smooth, which is why something just swallowed can be brought back up while a pill that has traveled farther down cannot.

  • Digestion – The digestive system breaks food into smaller nutrients that can be absorbed. This starts in the mouth and involves both mechanical and chemical processes.

    • Mechanical digestion includes physical breakdown: chewing in the mouth, churning in the stomach, and segmentation in the intestines—a rhythmic squeezing motion that mixes food with digestive juices and enhances absorption.

    • Chemical digestion breaks down food through enzymatic activity. Enzymes from the stomach, pancreas, and small intestine split large molecules like carbohydrates, proteins, and fats into smaller absorbable units.

  • Absorption – Small nutrients are absorbed into the blood for distribution to tissues. Most absorption occurs in the small intestine, though the stomach and large intestine also absorb certain substances.

  • Elimination – Indigestible material is eliminated as waste through defecation, a function of the large intestine.

The two major functional divisions of the digestive system are:

  • The alimentary canal (also called the gastrointestinal tract or GI tract) is a muscular tube from the mouth to the anus. It includes the mouth, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, and anus.

    The contents of the alimentary canal are technically outside the body. Nutrients become part of the body only after crossing the epithelial lining and entering blood vessels.

  • The accessory digestive organs lie outside the alimentary canal but aid digestion. They include the teeth, tongue, salivary glands, gallbladder, liver, and pancreas. They contribute materials—such as enzymes, bile, and saliva—into the canal rather than forming the tube itself.

Abdominal Quadrants and Regions

Clinicians divide the abdomen into four quadrants:

  • The right upper quadrant contains the liver, gallbladder, duodenum, and common bile duct.

  • The left upper quadrant contains the pancreas, stomach, and pancreatic duct.

  • The right lower quadrant contains the ascending colon, appendix, and small intestine.

  • The left lower quadrant contains part of the large and small intestine.

Quadrants provide a quick starting point for assessing injury or pain. For example, right lower quadrant tenderness may suggest appendicitis.

Anatomists further divide the abdomen into nine regions. The center region is the umbilical region. Superior to it is the epigastric region; inferior is the hypogastric region (also called the pubic region). Flanking the epigastric region are the left and right hypochondriac regions. Flanking the umbilical region are the left and right lumbar regions. Flanking the hypogastric region are the left and right iliac (inguinal) regions.

The names are landmark-derived. Hypo- means below and chondr- refers to cartilage, so the hypochondriac regions sit beneath the costal cartilages. The lumbar regions correspond to the lumbar vertebrae. Moving inferiorly from the lumbar regions leads to the ilia, giving the iliac regions their name.

Focus on understanding the 3×3 grid visually. Many organs span more than one region—the gallbladder, for example, lies at the border of the epigastric and right hypochondriac regions. The urinary bladder is found exclusively in the hypogastric region under normal conditions; superior extension into other regions indicates pathological overdistension.

Clinicians use quadrants for rapid symptom localization; anatomists use nine regions for precise organ mapping.

The alimentary canal wall consists of four layers. From deep to superficial:

  • The mucosa is the innermost layer, lining the lumen. It has three sublayers (deep to superficial):

    • The innermost lining is epithelium that varies along the tract; from the stomach through the intestines it is simple columnar epithelium. The mouth and esophagus are lined with nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium because they handle rough, unprocessed food. This layer is continuous with the ducts emptying into the lumen.

    • Just outside the epithelium is a layer of areolar connective tissue called the lamina propria, which provides vasculature for absorption.

    • The muscularis mucosae is a thin smooth muscle layer forming the outermost portion of the mucosa. It twitches rhythmically to dislodge particles stuck in the epithelial tissue.

  • The submucosa consists primarily of connective tissue rich in elastic fibers, which allow the wall to distend as food fills it and recoil once material passes. It also contains larger glands, blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerves.

  • The muscularis externa is the outer muscle layer, consisting of an inner circular layer and an outer longitudinal layer of smooth muscle. Circular layer contraction narrows the lumen; longitudinal layer contraction shortens the tube. Their coordinated action drives peristalsis and segmentation. The stomach adds a third, oblique layer (discussed later).

The mucosal epithelium of the GI tract is not ciliated. The thick muscular wall generates sufficient propulsive force without cilia—analogous to the inside of the heart, which also lacks cilia and moves fluid through muscular contraction.

  • The serosa is the outermost layer, corresponding to the visceral peritoneum. It is composed of simple squamous epithelium over a thin connective tissue layer. Where visceral peritoneum folds back to meet parietal peritoneum, it forms a double membrane called the mesentery. Organs behind the peritoneum (retroperitoneal) have an adventitia—a thicker connective tissue layer—instead of a serosa.

    The visceral peritoneum contacts organ surfaces directly; the parietal peritoneum lines the inner body wall. These two layers are continuous and fold back on themselves to create mesenteries. Any peritoneum not touching an organ is parietal, including the layers composing the mesenteries.

Mesenteries

Mesenteries are double layers of parietal peritoneum that anchor digestive organs, suspend them within the abdominal cavity, and provide a route for blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerves. Without these folds, organs would sag toward the lower abdomen. Each mesentery is named for the organ it attaches to.

Mesenteries serve three important functions:

  • They anchor organs in place.
  • They store adipose.
  • They provide a route for vessels to extend to and from the digestive organs.

Two types are dorsal and ventral mesenteries. A dorsal mesentery (the most common type) extends from the organ to the posterior abdominal wall. A ventral mesentery extends from the organ to the anterior abdominal wall. Both arise during embryonic development as the peritoneum wraps around the gut tube and its layers meet front and back.

The major dorsal mesenteries are:

  • The greater omentum, connecting the inferior stomach (greater curvature) to the posterior abdominal wall. Despite being the most ventral abdominal structure, it is classified as a dorsal mesentery because it hangs from the greater curvature, folds back on itself, and runs dorsally. It is the largest mesentery, resembles a lacy fat sheet, drapes over other organs, and is the first structure seen when the abdomen is opened.
  • The mesentery proper, associated with the majority of the small intestine, attaching to the dorsal body wall.
  • The transverse mesocolon, associated with the transverse colon.
  • The sigmoid mesocolon, connecting the sigmoid colon to the body wall.

The two ventral mesenteries are:

  • The falciform ligament, connecting the liver to the anterior abdominal wall and diaphragm.
  • The lesser omentum, connecting the liver to the lesser curvature of the stomach and the first part of the duodenum.

Together, these two mesenteries anchor the liver and stomach to the ventral body wall near the xiphoid process.

Intraperitoneal organs have mesenteries; retroperitoneal organs do not. The peritoneal cavity is the space between the visceral and parietal layers. The major intraperitoneal organs and their mesenteries are:

  • Liver – Lesser omentum and falciform ligament
  • Stomach – Greater and lesser omentum
  • Ileum and jejunum – Mesentery proper
  • Transverse colon – Transverse mesocolon
  • Sigmoid colon – Sigmoid mesocolon

Digestive organs alternate between intraperitoneal and retroperitoneal from stomach to rectum (the jejunum and ileum count as one unit; the duodenum stands alone):

  • Stomach – intraperitoneal
  • Duodenum – retroperitoneal
  • Jejunum and ileum – intraperitoneal
  • Ascending colon – retroperitoneal
  • Transverse colon – intraperitoneal
  • Descending colon – retroperitoneal
  • Sigmoid colon – intraperitoneal
  • Rectum – retroperitoneal

The pancreas is also retroperitoneal.

The mnemonic "SAD PUCKER" helps recall the retroperitoneal organs:

  • Suprarenal glands
  • Aorta/IVC
  • Duodenum (2nd–4th parts)
  • Pancreas (except tail)
  • Ureters

Primarily retroperitoneal organs (e.g., kidneys) develop behind the peritoneal cavity and were never enclosed by it. Secondarily retroperitoneal organs (e.g., pancreas, duodenum, parts of the colon) begin within the peritoneal cavity but become pressed against the dorsal body wall as the gut elongates, losing their mesentery and fusing to the posterior wall.

The Mouth, Pharynx, and Esophagus

The oral cavity (or mouth) is a mucosa-lined cavity composed of thick, nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium. It is bounded by the labia (lips), cheeks, palate, and tongue.

The tongue is composed primarily of skeletal muscle, allowing voluntary movement for speaking and manipulating food. Many movements during chewing and swallowing are reflexive. The tongue is nicknamed the "Great Dodger" because, despite its position between the teeth, it is rarely bitten during normal function.

  • The uvula swings back to close the opening between the oropharynx and nasopharynx, preventing liquids from entering the nasal cavity. It also participates in the gag reflex. Its surface is covered by nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium.

  • The fauces are the archways forming a narrow passage from the mouth to the pharynx between the base of the tongue and the soft palate. The palatine tonsils lie on the sides of the fauces. Think of the fauces as the oral counterpart of the internal nares—the passageway from the oral cavity into the oropharynx.

  • The hard palate forms the anterior roof of the mouth, composed of the right and left maxillae anteriorly and the right and left palatine bones posteriorly. The palatine raphe runs along the midline marking palatal fusion. Posterior to it is the soft palate, a flexible muscular extension terminating as the uvula. Together they separate the oral cavity from the nasal cavity.

  • The labial frenulum is a medial fold connecting the lips to the gums. The anatomical term for the gums is gingiva (singular) or gingivae (plural). Teeth are held by the maxilla and mandible in sockets called alveoli.

  • The lingual frenulum anchors the tongue to the floor of the mouth. If it extends too far toward the tip, it limits tongue protrusion—a condition known as ankyloglossia, or being "tongue-tied." In newborns this may interfere with nursing but can be corrected by clipping the frenulum.

  • The oral vestibule is the space between the teeth and the cheeks or lips.

  • At the base of the lingual frenulum are the openings of the submandibular ducts. Lateral to those are the openings of the sublingual ducts. The submandibular glands sit near the angle of the mandible; the sublingual glands lie beneath each side of the tongue and drain through multiple small ducts. Some individuals can voluntarily spray saliva from these ducts—a phenomenon called gleeking—by compressing the submandibular ducts with the floor-of-mouth muscles.

  • The parotid gland is the largest salivary gland. It sits superficially just in front of the ear. The parotid duct crosses the cheek, pierces the buccinator muscle, and opens onto the inner cheek surface near the upper teeth.

  • The salivary glands are accessory digestive organs. Saliva contains enzymes that aid taste and digestion and helps protect teeth from damaging substances. A healthy individual produces approximately 1.5 liters of saliva per day, most of which is swallowed and reabsorbed.

The Tongue

The tongue is the first major muscular organ of mechanical digestion. It consists of two kinds of muscles covered by epithelial tissue bearing taste buds. The epithelium regenerates well after minor burns or abrasion.

The tongue is divided into two parts:

  • The posterior one-third lies in the oropharynx. The boundary with the oral cavity is the V-shaped sulcus terminalis (terminal sulcus). The bumps on this region represent the lingual tonsil, a collection of lymphatic tissue—not papillae or taste buds. The lingual tonsil is not visible when looking in the mouth under normal conditions.

  • The anterior two-thirds lie in the oral cavity proper and bear visible bumps called papillae. Taste buds are microscopic structures embedded in the sides of papillae; food must dissolve in saliva and reach those creases to stimulate them.

      Filiform papillae are white papillae with no associated taste buds. They are the most abundant papillae and give the tongue its whitish appearance. They form posteriorly pointing spikes that grip material during licking. Cats have especially large filiform papillae, producing their characteristically rough tongue texture.

Taste information is carried to the brain by three cranial nerves: the facial nerve (CN VII) transmits taste from the anterior two-thirds; the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) carries taste from the posterior one-third; the vagus nerve (CN X) conveys taste from the epiglottis and nearby throat regions.

The classic "tongue map" showing separate regions for each taste is incorrect. All areas of the tongue—and even the epiglottis—contain receptors capable of detecting every basic taste.

The two kinds of tongue muscles:

  • Internal muscles of the tongue are attached to external muscles but not to bone or cartilage. They change the shape of the tongue (e.g., curling).

  • External muscles of the tongue are attached to the hyoid bone and skull bones. They change the position of the tongue in the oral cavity. The genioglossus muscle is one example; anchored anteriorly to the mandible, it fans back toward the epiglottis and its constant tone holds the tongue forward and out of the airway. Excessive relaxation of this muscle during sleep causes the tongue to slide back and occlude the airway—a common mechanism in sleep apnea.

Pharynx

The pharynx moves food from the oral cavity to the esophagus. Of its three sections (nasopharynx, oropharynx, laryngopharynx), only the oropharynx and laryngopharynx serve the digestive system.

During swallowing, muscles contract in sequence: one group lifts the larynx beneath the epiglottis to seal the airway; a second group squeezes the pharyngeal walls to push food into the esophagus; a third group lowers the larynx to reopen the airway. The airway is sealed mainly by lifting—the epiglottis tips posteriorly as underlying structures are pulled upward, and pharyngeal constrictors then squeeze the bolus past.

The Esophagus

The esophagus is a collapsible muscular tube extending from the laryngopharynx to the stomach, running through the thoracic cavity. Most of it is covered by an outer adventitia; only the short abdominal segment has a serosal covering.

At rest the esophagus is collapsed and its lumen is flat. Its mucosa is therefore folded into rugae that unfold rather than tear when the wall expands during swallowing.

The muscular wall transitions from skeletal to smooth muscle moving distally: the top one-third is skeletal (voluntary control); the middle one-third is mixed; the bottom one-third is smooth (involuntary). Because the upper esophagus contains skeletal muscle, food can sometimes be voluntarily moved back into the mouth if it has not traveled far.

At the transition into the stomach, the cardiac sphincter marks an abrupt change in epithelium: nonkeratinized stratified squamous above becomes simple columnar below.

In the mediastinum, the esophagus lies behind the trachea against the trachealis muscle. Because the trachealis can stretch, it allows the esophagus to bulge forward as a large bolus passes.

The esophagus passes through the diaphragm at the esophageal hiatus. The diaphragm also contains the aortic hiatus (aorta) and the vena caval hiatus (inferior vena cava).

The Stomach

The stomach is the widest portion of the alimentary canal, located in the abdominal cavity. It is the first site of protein breakdown and stores food for approximately four hours. During this time, churning mixes food with gastric acid and enzymes, converting it to a thick, partly digested paste called chyme.

Gross Anatomy

The stomach is J-shaped, bounded by the concave lesser curvature and the convex greater curvature. The cardiac region lies near the cardiac sphincter controlling flow from the esophagus. The fundus is the dome-shaped region superior to the cardiac region; it contains a high density of enzyme-producing gastric glands whose secretions mix thoroughly with stomach contents.

As the stomach approaches the duodenum it narrows into the pyloric region and pyloric canal. The pyloric sphincter controls flow into the duodenum. Both cardiac and pyloric sphincters are rings of smooth muscle formed by thickening of the circular muscularis externa layer.

When the cardiac sphincter fails to close tightly, stomach acid flows backward into the esophagus—gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). The resulting burning sensation is felt in the chest ("heartburn") because the esophagus lies behind the heart.

Rugae are temporary mucosal folds that disappear when the stomach fills. (The plicae circulares in the small intestine are similar but permanent.) Rugae are irregular in the body and pyloric region but straighten into parallel ridges in the pyloric canal, channeling chyme toward the duodenum.

Unlike the rest of the alimentary canal, the stomach's muscularis externa has three muscle layers. From serosa to lumen: longitudinal, circular, and oblique. The oblique layer, deepest and closest to the lumen, enables more effective churning.

Tissue Transition at the Cardiac Sphincter

There is a sharp epithelial transition at the cardiac sphincter. The esophagus is lined with nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium (abrasion-resistant); the stomach is lined with simple columnar epithelium (suited for secretion and absorption). This transition is immediate.

Production of Pepsin

The stomach surface contains gastric pits, which are ducts leading to gastric glands that produce gastric juices. A gastric pore is the surface opening; the gastric pit is the duct beneath it; the secreting gland lies below. These exocrine glands project deep into the lamina propria.

Chief cells deep in the gland produce the inactive precursor pepsinogen. When pepsinogen contacts hydrochloric acid (HCl) secreted by parietal cells (lining the gland walls above the chief cells), it is converted to pepsin—the active proteolytic enzyme—which is then secreted into the stomach lumen to begin protein digestion.

The Small Intestine

The small intestine is the longest part of the alimentary canal and the primary site of enzymatic digestion and nutrient absorption. Most digestive enzymes found here are secreted by the pancreas. Nutrients become part of the internal environment only after crossing the epithelial lining into the bloodstream.

Chyme moves from the stomach through the pyloric sphincter and then through three segments:

  • The duodenum is the first and shortest part—a C-shaped retroperitoneal organ that lies against the posterior body wall.

  • The jejunum is the middle segment, typically located in the upper-left abdomen.

  • The ileum is the final, longest segment, located in the lower-right abdomen, leading into the large intestine. (The ileum is near the ilium bone but spelled differently.)

Contents move through via two mechanisms:

  • Segmentation – Circular, rhythmic contractions mix and propel chyme distally.
  • Peristalsis – Coordinated muscular contractions propel material along.

Three structural modifications increase surface area for absorption:

  • Plicae circulares are permanent circular mucosal folds—unlike the temporary rugae of the stomach. They greatly increase surface area and help direct chyme distally.

  • Villi are finger-like mucosal projections (~1 mm tall) covered with simple columnar epithelium. Blood capillaries and lacteals (lymphatic vessels) run through their cores, allowing absorbed nutrients to enter the circulation. The mucosal surface resembles shag carpeting when viewed with the naked eye.

  • Microvilli are microscopic projections on the apical surface of each columnar epithelial cell, further increasing absorption surface area.

Accessory Organs: The Liver, Gallbladder, and Pancreas

The Liver and Gallbladder

The liver is the largest gland in the body. It produces bile, a green liquid that emulsifies fats. Bile is stored in the gallbladder, a muscular sac on the underside of the liver's right lobe. The gallbladder's inner lining contains rugae allowing expansion; its green appearance reflects stored bile.

The liver is composed primarily of hepatocytes (epithelial-derived cells) that regenerate rapidly, explaining the liver's exceptional healing capacity.

The liver has four lobes: two large primary lobes (left and right) and two smaller lobes (the quadrate and caudate lobes). Two ligaments: the ligamentum teres hepatis (divides the left lobe into medial and lateral sections) and the falciform ligament (attaches the liver to the anterior body wall).

Bile drains from the liver's left and right lobes into the left and right hepatic ducts, then into the common hepatic duct. The common hepatic duct joins the cystic duct to form the common bile duct, which empties into the duodenum at the major duodenal papilla—a small visible bump where both the common bile duct and pancreatic duct open. A sphincter at this junction regulates the release of both bile and pancreatic secretions.

When the small intestine no longer needs bile, distal sphincters close and bile backs up through the cystic duct into the gallbladder for storage. A small unidirectional valve prevents bile from returning to the liver.

Gallstones can obstruct the cystic duct, causing pain when the gallbladder contracts during a fatty meal. A gallstone at the major duodenal papilla is more dangerous because it blocks pancreatic enzyme release. People without a gallbladder are less efficient at digesting fat. Primary biliary cholangitis is an autoimmune condition that progressively destroys intrahepatic bile ducts, eventually causing bile accumulation, liver damage, and cirrhosis.

The Pancreas

The pancreas is a gland with both exocrine and endocrine functions. Its exocrine function is producing the majority of enzymes for chemical digestion. Its endocrine function is secreting hormones (insulin and glucagon) to regulate blood glucose.

The pancreas has three sections: a large head nestled in the C-curve of the duodenum, an elongated body, and a pointed tail resting against the spleen.

The pancreatic duct runs through the pancreas, collecting secretions from smaller fractional ducts and delivering them to the major duodenal papilla. A smaller accessory duct provides an alternative route for enzymes to reach the duodenum if the primary route is obstructed—an example of functional redundancy.

Pancreatic insufficiency (as in pancreatic cancer) causes rapid emaciation because enzymes are no longer produced in adequate quantities for proper digestion and nutrient absorption.

The Large Intestine

The large intestine (commonly called the colon) receives material not absorbed by the small intestine. Its primary function is reabsorbing water and electrolytes. Chyme arriving through the ileocecal valve is liquid; by the time feces are eliminated, water reabsorption has made them much more solid. Without this reabsorption, continuous diarrhea and dehydration would result.

When the large intestine cannot reabsorb this water—as during diarrhea—significant fluid loss reduces blood volume and impairs nutrient delivery throughout the body.

Unlike the constant peristalsis of the stomach and small intestine, the large intestine uses mass peristaltic movement—infrequent, powerful contractions that purge accumulated material. This is triggered when the cecum becomes distended, activating the nervous system to initiate a coordinated peristaltic wave.

Gross Anatomy of the Large Intestine

Chyme from the ileum enters the cecum through the ileocecal valve, which is stretch-dependent: as the cecum fills, increasing distension physically compresses and closes the valve. The vermiform appendix is attached to the cecum and suspended by a small mesentery.

From the cecum, contents travel up the ascending colon, turn at the right colic flexure (also called the right hepatic flexure, near the liver), cross as the transverse colon, turn at the left colic flexure (also called the left splenic flexure, near the spleen), descend as the descending colon, curve through the S-shaped sigmoid colon, and enter the rectum, which funnels into the anal canal and ends at the anus.

Specialized features of the large intestine:

  • Teniae coli are three longitudinal thickenings of the muscularis externa's outer layer that run along the colon's exterior. Contraction shortens the large intestine and creates haustra.

  • A haustrum (plural: haustra) is a bulge formed by the tension of the teniae coli, giving the large intestine its bumpy appearance.

  • Epiploic appendages are fat-filled pouches of visceral peritoneum hanging from the intestines. Their function is not well understood.

The large intestine also has abundant goblet cells producing mucus that lubricates passage of waste. As material becomes more solid distally, goblet cell numbers increase to reduce friction and protect the intestinal wall from tearing.

The Rectum, Anus, and Anal Sphincters

The rectum is retroperitoneal. It narrows distally into the anal canal. The pectinate line is a distinct demarcation near the distal anal canal. Superior to it, the mucosal lining forms longitudinal ridges called anal columns that channel waste toward the exit.

The rectal valves are transverse mucosal and muscular folds within the rectum that regulate passage of fecal matter and gas, maintaining continence by allowing gas to pass while retaining solid waste.

The anus marks the transition from the mucosal lining (simple columnar epithelium) to skin (keratinized stratified squamous epithelium).

Two sphincters control anal canal outflow:

  • The internal anal sphincter is a thickening of the circular muscularis externa, composed of smooth muscle—not under voluntary control.
  • The external anal sphincter consists of skeletal muscle under voluntary control.

The levator ani muscle lifts the anus during defecation to allow smoother passage of waste.

When the rectum fills, stretch receptors signal the spinal cord, triggering a reflex that contracts the rectal walls and relaxes the internal anal sphincter. Voluntary defecation occurs when motor neurons to the external anal sphincter are voluntarily inhibited, allowing feces to pass.

Clinical Correlation: Hemorrhoids

A hemorrhoid is a swollen vein within the mucosal lining or submucosa of the anus. These veins become inflamed because they are varicose veins with weakened walls and valves. Two types:

  • External hemorrhoids are inferior to the pectinate line. They are very painful but tend not to bleed much.
  • Internal hemorrhoids are superior to the pectinate line. They are typically less painful but bleed considerably.

A common treatment for symptomatic internal hemorrhoids is rubber-band ligation, in which a small band is placed at the base of the swollen vein to cut off its blood supply so it shrivels and falls off.

Blood color in stool indicates bleeding location: bright red blood suggests distal large intestine or rectal bleeding (e.g., hemorrhoids); black or tarry stool indicates higher GI bleeding where blood has been digested.
