An Introduction to Species & Macroevolution

Species Concepts

ConceptDefinition
Biological species concept (Mayr) Natural populations that actually or potentially interbreed and are reproductively isolated from other groups.
Morphological species concept Species defined by body shape, size, and structural features.
Lineage species concept Species share a branch on the tree of life; often based on DNA analysis.

Limitations of biological species concept:

  • Does not apply to asexual organisms (e.g., microbes).
  • Cannot be applied to fossils (morphological concept used instead).
  • "Potentially interbreed" is vague — hybrids (e.g., polar bear × grizzly = "pizzly") blur boundaries.

Reproductive Barriers

Prezygotic Barriers (prevent fertilization)

TypeMechanism
Habitat isolationPopulations never contact each other due to spatial separation.
Temporal isolationMating periods do not overlap (e.g., wood frog = late March; leopard frog = early April).
Behavioral isolationIncompatible mating rituals/signals (e.g., crab claw-wave patterns).
Mechanical isolationIncompatible reproductive structures (e.g., different petal sizes in sage).
Gametic isolationSperm and egg fail to fuse.

Postzygotic Barriers (after fertilization)

TypeMechanism
Reduced hybrid survivorshipOffspring are not viable; die soon after birth.
Reduced hybrid fertilityOffspring live but are sterile (e.g., mule = horse × donkey; odd chromosome number).

The Formation of New Species

Allopatric Speciation

  • Definition: Geographic barrier divides a population, cutting off gene flow; populations diverge independently.
  • Most common form of speciation, especially in animals. ("Allopatric" = other country.)
  • Examples: Drosophila on Hawaiian islands; antelope squirrels on opposite sides of a canyon.

Dobzhansky-Muller Model

  • Population splits → isolated groups accumulate different mutations at different loci.
  • Mutant allele from group A becomes incompatible with mutant allele from group B → reproductive isolation.
  • Also applies to chromosomal rearrangements (e.g., centric fusions in Rhogeessa bats → chromosomes can't pair in meiosis).
  • Reproductive isolation increases with genetic divergence, but even small divergence in "key" genes can isolate populations rapidly.

Sympatric Speciation

  • Definition: New species arise within the same geographic area; no physical barrier. ("Sympatric" = same country.)
  • More common in plants; increasingly documented in animals.

Two main causes:

CauseMechanismKey Details
Polyploidy Error in cell division → cells have extra genome copies; individual is reproductively isolated from diploids.
  • Autopolyploidy: extra sets from same species.
  • Allopolyploidy: extra sets from different species.
  • Tetraploid × diploid → triploid offspring (unbalanced chromosomes; evolutionary dead end).
  • Dominant mode of speciation in plants; rare in animals (amphibians = major exception; e.g., Hyla versicolor tetraploid tree frog).
Behavioral change Behavioral shift cuts off gene flow within same area.
  • Apple maggot flies: shifted from hawthorn to apple; disruptive selection → reproductive isolation.
  • Sockeye salmon: olfactory homing (Art Hasler experiment — nasal plugs disoriented fish); wrong-creek returns found new populations.
  • Cichlid fishes: hundreds of species in same African lake via resource/habitat partitioning.

Adaptive Radiation

  • Definition: Rapid evolution of many diverse species from a common ancestor under new environmental conditions.
  • Examples: Darwin's finches (Galápagos); cichlids (African lakes); Drosophila (Hawaiian islands); Hawaiian silversword plants (~35 species).

Coevolution & the Red Queen Hypothesis

  • Coevolution: Evolutionary influence shared between two interacting species (e.g., acacia tree ↔ acacia ants).
  • Red Queen hypothesis: Because species coevolve, each species must continually evolve just to maintain current fitness relative to the other — neither gains ground; both run to stay in place.

Key Takeaways

  • Know all three species concepts (biological, morphological, lineage) and the limitations of each.
  • Biological species concept does not apply to asexual organisms or fossils.
  • Prezygotic barriers prevent fertilization; postzygotic barriers reduce hybrid viability or fertility.
  • Allopatric speciation is the most common form, especially in animals — geographic barrier required.
  • The Dobzhansky-Muller model explains speciation via incompatible mutations accumulating independently in isolated populations.
  • Chromosomal rearrangements (e.g., centric fusions) can also drive reproductive isolation per the D-M model.
  • Sympatric speciation by polyploidy is the dominant mode in plants; rare in animals (amphibians are the exception).
  • Tetraploid × diploid → triploid = evolutionary dead end (unbalanced gametes).
  • Autopolyploidy = same-species extra sets; allopolyploidy = different-species extra sets.
  • Sympatric speciation via behavioral change: apple maggot flies, sockeye salmon, cichlids are canonical examples.
  • Art Hasler's nasal-plug experiment proved salmon use olfaction for homing — behavioral divergence can drive sympatric speciation.
  • Adaptive radiation: burst of speciation from one ancestor in new environments (finches, cichlids, Drosophila, silverswords).
  • Red Queen hypothesis (EXAM): coevolving species must continually evolve just to maintain current relative fitness — no net advantage gained.
  • Reproductive isolation increases with genetic divergence, but small divergence in key genes can still cause rapid isolation.
  • Both allopatric and sympatric speciation ultimately require a barrier to gene flow leading to genetic differentiation.
