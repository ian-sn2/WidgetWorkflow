Conservation Biology

Conservation biology is an integrated science that draws on many branches of biology to sustain biological diversity at every level. It examines whatever causes biodiversity to persist, decline, or recover.

Some call conservation biology a "discipline with a deadline" because remedial action is urgently needed for many species threatened by extinction. Threatened species include lions in Sub-Saharan Africa, short-beaked dolphins, leatherback turtles, and forest elephants.

Conservation ecologists are concerned about species diversity, genetic diversity, and ecosystem diversity. Genetic diversity is a major concern because when populations dwindle and genetic diversity falls, extinction becomes more likely.

Genetic diversity comes down to alleles—the alternative versions of a gene. The more alleles a population holds, the better its odds that some individuals can withstand a new disease, climate shift, or habitat change. Once an allele disappears, only a new mutation or immigrant can restore it. Our food supply illustrates the danger: roughly 95% of U.S. apple varieties grown in 1900 no longer exist, and in 1970 a single fungus devastated the U.S. corn crop because most fields were genetically too similar to resist it.

Small, isolated populations are especially prone to extinction due to: genetic drift (alleles vanish by chance), inbreeding depression (offspring of related parents survive and reproduce poorly), small effective population size, stochastic events such as storms or disease outbreaks, and too little genetic variation to adapt to change.

A well-known example of genetic rescue involves the Florida panther. By the 1990s, the population had declined to about 20–30 individuals, suffering severe inbreeding and genetic defects. In 1995, conservationists introduced eight female pumas from Texas, increasing gene flow and genetic diversity. The population has since rebounded to about 200 individuals, demonstrating how introducing new individuals can restore genetic variation in small, isolated populations.

Ecology in the News: Relocated Lynx and a Lethal Gene

Moving animals does not always rescue a population. According to a study published in November 2026, a new Eurasian lynx population in northeastern Switzerland was started in the early 2000s with just 12 animals, three of which carried a harmful recessive allele of a gene called FGF23. Genetic drift and inbreeding pushed the carrier frequency from roughly 10% in the source population to about half in the new one. Lynx inheriting two copies die young as minerals accumulate in their organs.

Unlike the Florida panther rescue, which added alleles, this relocation concentrated a damaging one—a reminder that founder groups should be large and genetically varied. A separate project in Slovenia and Croatia is doing just that, bringing in 18 lynx from the Carpathian Mountains to add new genes to an inbred population.

Pablo Escobar's Hippos

In the 1980s, Colombian drug lord Pablo Escobar illegally imported four hippos to his private zoo at Hacienda Nápoles. After Escobar's death in 1993, the hippos were left behind and escaped into surrounding river systems. The population has since grown to over 100 individuals.

This example highlights the problem of small founding populations: because only four founders existed, many descendants are closely related, increasing inbreeding depression and accumulation of harmful traits. Even with population growth, genetic health remains compromised by the limited founding diversity.

Hippos are also considered ecosystem engineers—their presence significantly alters ecosystem structure and function, contributing to ecological disruption.

Major Threats to Biodiversity

The rate of extinction in recent decades has been as much as 1,000 times the background rate, leading many scientists to call the current loss the "sixth mass extinction." One estimate suggests animal populations have decreased by about half in the last 48 years.

Major threats to biodiversity include:

  • Habitat destruction – The most important threat to biodiversity. Humans have already degraded approximately 40–50% of Earth's terrestrial habitats. One estimate suggests we lose an acre of rainforest every 2 minutes.

  • Invasive and introduced species – Organisms moved—intentionally or unintentionally—into regions where they did not previously exist. They often lack natural predators, spread rapidly, outcompete native species, and disrupt food webs. Examples include zebra mussels in the Great Lakes and Burmese pythons in the Florida Everglades, which have dramatically reduced native mammal populations. The dodo's extinction illustrates how species evolving in predator-free island environments are especially vulnerable: invasive rats, pigs, and monkeys destroyed eggs and competed for resources.

  • Overexploitation – Also called overharvesting. About 14% of fisheries have collapsed due to overfishing; ivory hunters have similarly overexploited African elephant populations.

  • Pollution – Exponential human population growth and fossil fuel use have caused significant air pollution, disrupting nutrient cycles. DDT pushed the bald eagle toward extinction; Rachel Carson's Silent Spring publicized the damage, contributing to the creation of the U.S. EPA. Pharmaceuticals excreted by humans also pollute waterways.

  • Climate change – Human activities have driven global warming, threatening species unable to shift their ranges fast enough. The key issue is not just that conditions are changing but the unusually rapid rate of change. Over the past ~10,000 years, relatively stable climate allowed ecosystems to develop under predictable conditions. Current CO₂-driven shifts in temperature and precipitation outpace evolutionary adaptation timescales, increasing extinction risk.

Case Study: Cheetahs

Cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus) were once widespread across Africa and Asia, but human actions have reduced populations by more than 90% since 1900. The global population is now only about 7,100 individuals, almost entirely in Africa with a tiny remnant in Iran.

Key questions a conservation biologist must address include:

  • What are cheetahs' interactions with and competition against other species?
  • Where do cheetahs live, and what is their home range?
  • What do cheetahs eat, and what are their life history traits?
  • What are cheetahs' social systems and human attitudes toward them?
  • What diseases are cheetahs susceptible to, and how are they transmitted?
  • What is the genetic diversity, age structure, and sex ratio of the population?
  • How fast do cheetah populations grow, and what are extinction probabilities?

We lack answers to many of these questions, demonstrating that conservation biology is a "crisis science."

Global cheetah populations have low genetic diversity—revealed when a skin transplant between an East African and South African cheetah was not rejected. The cause is a population bottleneck in the late Pleistocene, when warmer, drier climate shrank the cheetah's range; numbers later recovered, but lost alleles did not. Low genetic diversity leads to inbreeding depression, low sperm counts and motility, low female fertility, poor offspring survivorship, and reduced evolutionary potential.

Another problem is the "supermom" phenomenon: in the southern Serengeti, a single female was responsible for 10% of all cheetahs in the area. This reproductive skew means fewer individuals contribute genes to the next generation, reducing effective population size and increasing inbreeding and genetic drift risk.

Additional threats include competition with lions and hyenas (killing up to 95% of cheetah cubs) and the wildlife trade—it is reportedly popular to keep cheetahs as pets in Saudi Arabia.

Effective Population Size

Not all individuals in a population breed; some never pass genes to future generations. Effective population size measures how many individuals are, in effect, sharing equally in passing genes to the next generation. It depends only on breeding individuals and their relative proportions, not total population size. When reproduction is uneven or sex ratios are skewed, effective population size can be far smaller than actual population size, reducing genetic diversity and amplifying genetic drift. Past bottlenecks also lower effective population size.

The equation for effective population size is:

\[ \text{Effective Population Size } (N_e) = \frac{4N_mN_f}{N_m + N_f} \]

where . . .

  • \(N_m\) = The number of breeding males in the population
  • \(N_f\) = The number of breeding females in the population

Example 1: A population of 16 cheetahs with 5 breeding males and 2 breeding females:

\[ \text{Effective Population Size} = \frac{4N_mN_f}{N_m + N_f} = \frac{4(5)(2)}{5 + 2} = \frac{40}{7} = 5.71 \]

Example 2: Effective population size equals actual size when breeding is equal—8 males and 8 females from a population of 16:

\[ \text{Effective Population Size} = \frac{4N_mN_f}{N_m + N_f} = \frac{4(8)(8)}{8 + 8} = \frac{256}{16} = 16 \]

An extreme skew sets a hard ceiling: with just one breeding male, the effective population size stays below 4 regardless of how many females breed.

Quiz

A wildlife refuge has a total population of 200 Florida panthers, but because of territorial dominance, only 30 males are able to reproduce in each generation. Based on the idea of effective population size, what is the most likely long-term consequence for this population?

a. The effective population size will remain 200, so the population should retain strong genetic diversity
b. The effective population size will rise above 200 because nearby populations will offset the imbalance
c. The effective population size will be far below 200, making the population more vulnerable to genetic drift
d. The effective population size will be 30, but this will not meaningfully affect the population's genetics
e. The population will quickly become better adapted because only the strongest males reproduce

Because only a limited portion of the population is actually contributing genes to the next generation, the effective population size is much smaller than the census size of 200. A smaller effective population size increases the effects of genetic drift, which can reduce genetic variation over time and increase inbreeding risk. Even if the total headcount seems fairly large, unequal reproductive success can make the population genetically fragile. For instance, if the other 170 panthers were all breeding females, the effective population size would be only about 102.

Metapopulations

Many organisms live in habitat patches separated by unsuitable environments, forming subpopulations linked by dispersal. Collectively, these linked subpopulations form a metapopulation. The 33 cheetah subpopulations in southern Africa, some close enough for gene flow, illustrate this structure.

Because individuals move in and out, each subpopulation is an open population described by the BIDE model (births, immigration, deaths, emigration), rather than the BD model (births and deaths only) used for closed populations.

Two types of subpopulations within a metapopulation:

  • A source population is one in which births outnumber deaths, so the per capita growth rate is positive and the population is growing.
  • A sink population is one in which deaths outnumber births, so the per capita growth rate is negative and the population is shrinking.

The rate of colonization depends primarily on distance from a source; the rate of extinction depends primarily on habitat size. Sink populations survive only because immigrants keep arriving from sources; protecting source habitat is therefore usually the priority. A human-created sink can sometimes be restored to a source by fixing the underlying cause. However, protecting habitat that can never support a self-sustaining population creates an ecological trap: animals keep moving in, only to die.

Two Approaches to Conservation

  • A species-specific approach – Attempts to save individual species from extinction. Examples include conservation efforts for the black rhino, manatee, and black-footed ferret. The Endangered Species Act (ESA) and the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES) embrace this approach. It is popular with donors who connect with charismatic species, but many species most in need of help—such as insects—lack that appeal.

  • A system-level approach – Attempts to save all species within a given ecosystem rather than targeting individual species. Examples include preserving sections of the Everglades or Afromontane forests. Adopted by The Nature Conservancy (TNC), this approach protects many species at once but is harder to "sell" to the public, may face uncooperative governments in biodiversity hotspots, and is more difficult to evaluate for success.

The Problem

Conservation biologists face a difficult challenge:

  • Almost half of Earth's terrestrial surface has been degraded or altered.
  • Extinction rates are as much as 1,000 times the background rate. Nearly 25% of mammals, 13% of birds, 18% of fish, and 42% of amphibians are endangered or threatened—what many scientists call the sixth mass extinction.

These pressures have prompted discussion of species triage—prioritizing which species to save and allowing others to go extinct, freeing resources for more recoverable species. This approach has been used in Australia and New Zealand but not yet in the United States. Supporters argue it frees money for winnable battles; critics counter that difficult rescues like the California condor succeeded precisely because triage was not applied.

Why Should You Care about Conservation Biology?

  1. Biological diversity financially benefits humans. Diverse organisms have yielded new chemicals, foods, and medicines; 70% of medicines developed in the last 25 years came from nature. Bioprospecting refers to the search for biological organisms that can be commercialized for medical or industrial purposes. The rosy periwinkle from Madagascar, for example, produces compounds used to treat leukemia and Hodgkin's lymphoma.

  2. Biological diversity maintains important ecosystem services. Humans depend on ecosystems for clean air, clean water, nitrogen fixation, UV protection, and flood control. Services are grouped as provisioning (food, water, fiber, medicine), regulating (climate, air/water quality, flood control), and cultural (recreation, beauty, study). Ecosystem services are estimated at $44 trillion per year.

  3. Other reasons – Biological diversity is aesthetically pleasing, explaining widespread human interest in nature and wildlife.

Conservation can work: Florida panthers have climbed from fewer than 30 to about 200, bald eagles from 417 nesting pairs in the lower 48 states (1963) to roughly 71,400 (2020), and California condors from 22 birds (1982) to more than 500. Each recovery required legal protection, funding, and decades of effort.

Quiz

The clearing of 10,000 hectares of tropical rainforest removes a large area of mature forest. Which set of ecological outcomes would a community ecologist most likely expect to occur over the following decades?

a. An increase in primary productivity and a rise in overall species diversity
b. A rapid return to the original forest community within about five years
c. Higher effective population sizes across all remaining species
d. A spread of K-selected species into the disturbed environment
e. Loss of carbon storage, disruption of food webs (trophic downgrading), and weaker regulation of water systems

Removing old-growth forest reduces the ecosystem's ability to store carbon, disrupts food webs through trophic downgrading, and weakens watershed regulation. Recovery is typically slow and dominated by early successional species, not a rapid return to the original complex community.
