Introduction to the Urinary System

The urinary system eliminates wastes and regulates the chemical constancy and volume of blood, maintaining an appropriate balance of water, acids, electrolytes, and bases. It also plays an important role in regulating blood pressure.

The most important organs of the urinary system are the kidneys. The right kidney sits lower than the left because the liver pushes it down. The adrenal glands sit atop the kidneys but are not part of the urinary system. The kidneys are located in the superior lumbar region of the posterior abdominal wall, deep to ribs 11 and 12 (floating ribs), which provide only partial bony protection.

Extending medially from each kidney are the ureters, muscular tube-like structures that funnel urine into the urinary bladder. Urine is stored there until expelled through the urethra. As the ureters descend, they pass anterior to the psoas major muscle and cross anterior to the external iliac artery and vein.

Beyond urine formation, the kidneys constantly regulate solute concentrations and help maintain stable blood volume by filtering and adjusting the chemical makeup of the blood. The water used to make urine comes directly from blood plasma.

How Well Protected Is the Kidney?

Because the kidneys are retroperitoneal, they lie directly against the posterior abdominal wall without the cushioning of the peritoneal cavity or mesenteries, making them more vulnerable to injury than fully enclosed organs. The brain is protected by the skull; the heart and lungs by the ribcage and sternum. In contrast, the kidneys are covered only by thinner musculature and floating ribs.

Kidney trauma ranges from a surface bruise (from a direct blow) to a laceration or, in major trauma such as a car accident, a torn vascular attachment. Together, the kidneys receive roughly 20% of cardiac output at rest, so deep lacerations or torn attachments can cause rapid internal bleeding. Kidney punches are illegal in boxing for this reason.

The kidneys have several compensatory protections: the renal capsule (also called the fibrous capsule), a layer of perirenal fat, and anterior and posterior bands of renal fascia that help keep the kidney in place.

Gross Anatomy of the Kidney

A frontal section reveals a light outer portion called the renal cortex and a striped inner portion called the renal medulla. The cortex is anchored into the medulla by extensions called renal columns.

The dark, triangular areas in the medulla are renal pyramids. The renal medulla consists of both the renal pyramids and columns. The pointy ends of each pyramid are the renal papillae. Each papilla drains into a minor calyx; multiple minor calyces combine to form a major calyx. The calyces drain into the renal pelvis, which narrows into the ureter.

The renal pelvis, major calyces, blood vessels, nerves, and fat exist inside a cavity called the renal sinus. Blood vessels and nerves enter through the renal hilus (also called the renal hilum). Students often confuse these terms: the renal sinus is the internal cavity containing fat, vessels, and the renal pelvis; the renal pelvis is the funnel-shaped collecting region; and the renal hilum is the external indentation where vessels and the ureter enter or exit.

EXAM TIP: Structural Terminology

Students often confuse the terms renal pelvis, renal sinus, and renal hilum. Be sure to understand the difference between these structures.

Microscopic Anatomy of the Kidney

The nephron is the smallest functional unit of the kidney. It consists of two parts: the renal corpuscle and a tubular section.

  • Renal corpuscle – Exists in the cortex only. It consists of a tuft of fenestrated capillaries called the glomerulus, surrounded by a cup-shaped capsule called the glomerular capsule (or Bowman's capsule).

The outer wall of Bowman's capsule is simple squamous epithelium (the parietal layer). As it approaches the glomerulus, the epithelium transitions to specialized podocytes, which form the visceral layer. Podocyte processes interdigitate and act as a strainer: filtered material passes through capillary fenestrations and intercellular clefts, then through slits between podocyte processes. Glomerular capillaries are fenestrated because clefts alone would not allow material out quickly enough.

The filtration membrane consists of all the layers fluid must pass through. During filtration, material leaves the blood through the capillary wall, crosses the visceral layer, and collects in the capsular space—which is continuous with the proximal convoluted tubule. Whatever remains in the tubule lumen becomes urine; whatever is reabsorbed into the peritubular capillaries returns to the body.

  • Tubular section – Consists of the proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, and collecting duct.

All nephrons have renal corpuscles in the cortex. Juxtamedullary nephrons have loops of Henle extending deep into the medulla; cortical nephrons have loops ending near the corticomedullary junction. About 90–95% of nephrons are cortical, but juxtamedullary nephrons are more important to urine production and are the focus of study. In both types, the corpuscle stays in the cortex; only the loop of Henle dips into the medulla.

Blood enters the glomerulus through the afferent arteriole and exits through the efferent arteriole. No gas exchange occurs in the glomerulus, so blood leaving it is still arterial. The efferent arteriole contains more smooth muscle than a venous structure, allowing pressure regulation into the peritubular capillaries. The afferent arteriole is usually slightly wider than the efferent arteriole.

From the glomerulus, filtrate flows through the proximal convoluted tubule, then the descending limb of the loop of Henle, the ascending limb, the distal convoluted tubule, and finally the collecting duct. As the collecting duct approaches the renal papilla, it is called the papillary duct. Urine leaving the papillary duct into a minor calyx is final and can no longer be modified. It then flows through major calyces → renal pelvis → ureter → bladder → urethra.

The tubular section is surrounded by blood vessels. The peritubular capillaries extend from the efferent arteriole and cling to the proximal and distal convoluted tubules in the cortex, eventually emptying into veins. The vasa recta are specialized, highly ordered capillaries associated with the loops of Henle of juxtamedullary nephrons—a more specific subtype of peritubular capillaries with a distinct structural arrangement.

EXAM TIP: Vasa Recta Association

Remember that the vasa recta are only associated with the loops of Henle of juxtamedullary nephrons.

The kidney's blood supply includes two capillary beds in sequence—glomerular capillaries followed by peritubular capillaries (or vasa recta)—an arrangement called a portal system. The peritubular capillaries of cortical nephrons form a typical convoluted bed, while the vasa recta of juxtamedullary nephrons are long, straight, and parallel to the nephron.

Proximal convoluted tubule cells have abundant microvilli and mitochondria to support high reabsorptive activity. Distal convoluted tubule cells have few microvilli and fewer mitochondria but carry numerous receptors that allow regulated, signal-driven movement of ions and water.

The Juxtaglomerular Apparatus

The juxtaglomerular apparatus (also called the juxtaglomerular complex) is a point of contact between the afferent arteriole and the distal convoluted tubule, which loops back between the afferent and efferent arterioles. It helps regulate blood pressure by detecting filtrate flow changes and adjusting glomerular blood flow or releasing signaling molecules.

Its specialized cells include:

  • Macula densa cells – Modified epithelial cells in the distal tubule wall that sense sodium and chloride in the filtrate. High NaCl signals that filtration is excessive; the macula densa signals the afferent arteriole to constrict, reducing filtration. This built-in feedback loop regulates blood volume and pressure.
  • Granular cells – Modified smooth muscle cells in the afferent arteriole that secrete hormones causing the arteriole to dilate or constrict. They are most abundant in the afferent arteriole.

By controlling glomerular blood flow, these cells regulate urine production and, consequently, blood volume and pressure.

Basic Processes of Urine Production

Urine production occurs in three sequential steps:

  • Filtration – Blood pressure forces plasma through the fenestrated glomerular capillaries into Bowman's capsule. This is a relatively non-specific process; blood cells and proteins do not cross the filtration membrane. Only about 20% of plasma entering the glomerulus is filtered; the remaining ~80% exits via the efferent arteriole.
  • Reabsorption – Because filtration is non-selective, valuable substances (such as water) end up in the filtrate. The peritubular capillaries selectively reabsorb these and return them to the bloodstream.
  • Secretion – Substances not filtered may be selectively secreted from the peritubular capillaries into the tubular lumen.

The peritubular capillaries cooperate with the tubular nephron in reabsorption and secretion to determine the final volume and composition of urine.

The Ureters

Urine drains from the kidneys into the ureters, which extend from the renal pelvis (around L2) to the urinary bladder, passing over the sacroiliac joint region. Smooth muscle in the ureteral wall generates peristaltic waves to actively push urine toward the bladder. Because the ureters open at the base of the bladder, urine arriving later must be pushed in against the pressure of urine already there, requiring the ureter to generate higher pressure than the bladder.

Urine enters the bladder at an angle on the posterior-inferior wall; as the bladder fills, increasing internal pressure compresses the ureteral openings, preventing reflux. Despite common textbook depictions, the ureters drain into the bottom, posterior portion of the bladder—not the top. Ureters are typically about 10 inches long.

Kidney stones are jagged concretions that cause pain as they pass through the ureter and urethra. A stone is not felt in the spacious bladder lumen but causes pain again in the narrow urethra. Stones too large to pass may be broken up medically or retrieved surgically. Dr. Ahlgren described a case in which her father developed very large stones lodged in the renal pelvis that could not be removed and repeatedly caused bacterial infections leading to kidney failure, illustrating risks beyond pain alone.

The Urinary Bladder

Urine drains from the ureters into the urinary bladder via the ureteric orifices. The urinary bladder is a hollow organ whose wall is primarily smooth muscle; the outer layer is called the detrusor muscle.

The detrusor muscle is not under voluntary control; however, urination can be voluntarily regulated through the external urethral sphincter, made of skeletal muscle. The bladder lies directly behind the pubic symphysis, inferior to the peritoneal cavity—the peritoneum drapes over its top rather than enclosing it. The female bladder is somewhat smaller, with the uterus resting directly on top of it. When very full, the bladder can be palpated just above the pubic symphysis.

When examining pelvic models, always determine sex before identifying structures. The male urinary bladder is often mistaken for a uterus on lab models.

The Urethra

Urine drains from the bladder into the urethra. As with defecation, micturition (urination) is controlled by two sphincters:

  • The internal urethral sphincter is a thickening of smooth muscle at the base of the bladder wall, under autonomic (parasympathetic) control. Parasympathetic activity causes it to relax during urination.

  • The external urethral sphincter consists of voluntary skeletal muscle within the urogenital diaphragm—part of the pelvic floor—which contains openings for the urethra (and vagina in females). Contracting this sphincter is how you voluntarily "hold your pee."

Gender-based differences in urethral structure:

  • Males – The male urethra has three regions: the prostatic region (through the prostate), the short membranous region (through the urogenital diaphragm), and the long spongy region (through the corpus spongiosum of the penis), ending at the external urethral orifice. The internal and external sphincters are separated by the prostatic urethra.

    Both sexes have a trigone—a smooth triangular region formed by the two ureteral orifices and the internal urethral orifice—which is a common site for infections, since pathogens entering through the urethra can quickly reach and spread from this area. Both sexes also have internal rugae that disappear as the bladder fills.

  • Females – The female urethra is much shorter, is not divided into regions, and its sphincters are closer together. Urinary tract infections are more common in females due to the shorter urethra and its proximity to the anal region.

The pelvic floor works with the diaphragm to regulate abdominopelvic pressure. When the diaphragm contracts downward, the pelvic floor lifts upward, stabilizing the core—important for lifting and posture. In a superior view of the female pelvic floor, openings from posterior to anterior are the anus, vaginal orifice, and urethral orifice. Because these are skeletal muscles, they can be voluntarily strengthened; a weak pelvic floor can cause stress urinary incontinence (e.g., during coughing or sneezing).

The Process of Micturition

Micturition begins when bladder stretch activates sensory neurons that relay signals to the pontine micturition center in the pons, which integrates sensory input with higher brain signals.

The pontine micturition center coordinates urination by:

  • Inhibiting sympathetic activity (which normally keeps the bladder relaxed)
  • Inhibiting somatic motor neurons to the external urethral sphincter (allowing it to relax)
  • Activating parasympathetic motor neurons (contracting the detrusor muscle)

Urination requires three things:

  • Opening the external urethral sphincter
  • Opening the internal urethral sphincter
  • Squeezing the bladder walls to force urine out

At rest, sympathetic activity keeps the detrusor relaxed and internal sphincter closed. During urination, parasympathetic activity contracts the detrusor and relaxes the internal sphincter, while somatic motor inhibition relaxes the external sphincter. Bladder contraction is parasympathetic because urination is a "rest and digest" function.

Clinical Example: Polycystic Kidney Disease

Polycystic kidney disease is a genetic condition in which numerous fluid-filled cysts develop throughout the kidneys, causing them to become greatly enlarged and impairing their function. In severe cases, each diseased kidney can weigh roughly 11 pounds. A donor kidney is implanted first, and the diseased kidneys are removed once the transplant is functioning. Severe cases require dialysis and may ultimately need a kidney transplant, illustrating how structural changes can profoundly disrupt fluid regulation and waste removal.
