An Introduction to Species

Recall that we defined microevolution as a change in the frequencies of genotypes in a population. In this lecture, we will depart from the topic of microevolution and begin to talk about macroevolution, or the formation of species.

The Definition of a Species

A species is defined as a group of natural populations that either actually or potentially interbreed and are reproductively isolated from other such groups.

This definition of a species is known as the "biological species concept" and is attributed to Ernst Mayr, a scientist who spent his summers at the University of Florida. Mayr died in 2005 at the age of 100; he was active in the scientific community until his death. In fact, he published his last book at the age of 100!

Other Definitions of Species

In class, we discussed the biological species concept, which defines a species as a population or group of populations whose members have the potential to interbreed in nature and produce viable, fertile offspring, but are unable to produce viable, fertile offspring with members of other populations.

The textbook does, however, discuss a number of other species definitions with which you should be familiar, including the following:

  • The morphological species concept defines a species based on body shape, size, and other structural features.
  • The lineage species concept defines a species as organisms that share a branch on the tree of life. That is, organisms are a member of the same species if they are closely related to one another in terms of evolution. This is often based on DNA analysis.

While the morphological species concept definition of a species is useful, it does have some problems. For example, consider the meadowlark. Dr. Gillooly presented two pictures of virtually identical meadowlark birds. Although they look like each other, they are technically different species because they are reproductively isolated. On the other hand, consider groups of humans that look very different from one another, despite being the same species.

Some specific problems with our working definition of a species are as follows:

  • The definition does not apply to asexual organisms. For example, some microbes simply divide asexually and thus could not be classified based on species, using this definition. Microbial biologists tend to use physiology, ribosomal structure, and other mechanisms for defining a species.
  • The definition cannot be applied to fossils. Fossils do not necessarily tell us about the sexual isolation of different animals; they just tell us about the physiology of animals. For example, if you just had the fossils of two different meadowlarks, you would think that they were the same species (and the same animal). However, as we discussed previously, meadowlarks that look very similar can actually be classified as different species. The morphological species concept is the approach generally used when identifying fossil species.
  • The definition encompasses animals that actually or potentially interbreed, which includes a broad range of animals. The words "potentially interbreed" are not very well-defined, particularly in the case of hybrids. For example, if two organisms would never come into contact in nature but are able to breed when they are put together in a zoo, does that make them the same species? Often, two animals can technically reproduce, but the viability of the offspring is so low that the two animals are nevertheless considered different species. For example, consider polar bears and grizzly bears—two animals that we can readily identify as different species. Hybrids of these two animals have been found in the wild, where the bears' natural environments are beginning to overlap as those environments are disrupted, indicating that they can, in fact, interbreed. (The hybridized form is sometimes called a "pizzly bear," and it has features of both parents: a slightly darker coat than a polar bear and a face more like that of a grizzly bear.) It turns out that these hybrids are fertile. Strictly speaking, then, the biological species concept would not treat polar bears and grizzly bears as different species. Nevertheless, they are still classified as separate species.

Reproductive Barriers

Recall that the defining characteristic that separates two species is that they cannot interbreed. Reproductive barriers between species can come in two forms: prezygotic barriers (which prevent the formation of a zygote) and postzygotic barriers (which operate after a zygote has been formed). We will talk about both of these kinds of barriers.

Prezygotic barriers are reproductive barriers that prevent fertilization. Some examples of prezygotic barriers are as follows:

  • Habitat isolation – Some animals are isolated by space such that they never have the opportunity to come into contact with one another.
  • Temporal isolation – Some animals are isolated by time such that they never have the opportunity to come into contact with one another. For example, the wood frog's mating activity peaks at the end of March, and the leopard frog's mating activity peaks at the beginning of April. Even just a few weeks' difference in peak mating activity can prevent these species from interbreeding.
  • Behavioral isolation – Some animals have mating rituals that must occur before mating. Pairing of mates does not occur unless the correct mating rituals or signals are performed, even if the animals live in the same place. For example, two species of crabs on the coast of South Carolina look basically identical, but they are behaviorally isolated because they use different cues for reproduction. When these crabs want to mate, they stand up on a mound and wave their large claw in a certain way. This is the cue for other crabs to mate with them. One species waves its claw in a figure-eight pattern, while the other species waves its claw in a different motion (such as up and down); if a crab does not wave its claw in the way that its own species uses, the "mating cue" will not be sent.
  • Mechanical isolation – Some animals reproduce in ways that are simply not physically compatible with other animals. Dr. Gillooly described this kind of barrier as follows: "Your parts won't fit!" For example, two species of sage plants have different-sized lower petals, which are used as a landing platform for pollinators. The larger species relies on fertilization by large insects. These insects cannot land on the smaller species, so pollination is impossible.
  • Gametic isolation – Some animals produce sperm and egg cells that simply will not fuse when they come into contact. There are huge differences in the morphologies of sperm and eggs, even amongst amphibians. It is no wonder there is so much gametic isolation.

Note that these different kinds of prezygotic barriers are not exclusive; that is, two organisms may be reproductively isolated by multiple barriers at once. Moreover, one barrier alone may not totally isolate two groups of organisms. For example, consider the frog example described above, which we identified as temporal isolation. Although this is a key contributor to speciation in these frogs, temporal isolation alone is not enough to completely separate the populations. Another factor at work is behavioral isolation, as frogs use species-specific mating calls to attract mates.

Postzygotic barriers are reproductive barriers that operate after fertilization occurs. In the end, it is all about reproductive success: if a hybrid's lifetime reproductive success is lower, it cannot compete with non-hybrids, and its lineage is effectively an evolutionary dead end. The following are examples of postzygotic barriers:

  • Reduced hybrid survivorship – Sometimes, two animals are able to sexually reproduce and create offspring, but those offspring are not viable and thus die soon after birth. This obviously limits the hybrid's reproductive viability and effectively prevents successful mating between the two parents.
  • Reduced hybrid fertility – Some animals sexually reproduce to form offspring that can live but cannot reproduce. For example, when a male donkey and a female horse sexually reproduce, they create a mule. Mules can live a full lifetime, but they are sterile because they have a strange chromosome number.

The establishment of a barrier to gene flow allows populations to diverge through mutation (i.e., isolated populations differentiate through microevolution). Sometimes, animals that differentiate and subsequently "get back together" through the removal of the barrier to gene flow can interbreed. For example, different species of European and American sycamore trees can still interbreed, despite having been separated for over 20 million years, and yet they are still considered separate species. Likewise, as we have discussed, grizzly bears and polar bears can interbreed and produce fertile hybrids. Cases like these are why Mayr's definition refers to populations that "actually or potentially" interbreed: he knew that there was a gray area. Many times, however, differentiation through microevolution prevents future interbreeding between different organisms.

The Formation of New Species

The entire process of speciation is something that biologists are just starting to understand. In general, there are two kinds of speciation: allopatric speciation and sympatric speciation. We will talk about both of these below. Dr. Gillooly said that you will certainly see these two types of speciation on an exam.

Allopatric Speciation

Allopatric speciation occurs when a geographic barrier divides a population, or when part of a population crosses a geographic barrier and founds a new population that is separate and distinct from the original population. This is the most common form of speciation, especially in animals. The word "allopatric" literally means "other country."

For example, let's say that one kind of plant exists in mountains on both sides of a valley, and in the valley itself. For some reason, the valley is flooded and the plants on the different mountains are isolated from one another. Because the flood has cut off gene flow between the plants on both sides of the valley, the populations can differentiate independently, leading to physical and genotypic differences. How quickly this happens depends on a number of factors, including body size, temperature, and UV exposure.

Dr. Gillooly also presented the following examples of allopatric speciation:

  • Fruit flies – Drosophila fruit flies originated on a single Hawaiian island. However, they eventually were able to travel to all of the Hawaiian islands (carried there by events such as storms), where they became many different species. The geographic barriers between the different islands in Hawaii provided the Drosophila flies with environments in which they could differentiate. Gene flow was also cut off within individual islands, because some flies came to live on the dry side of an island while others lived on the wet side. Today, roughly 25% of all Drosophila species in the world are found on the Hawaiian islands. Speciation was this rapid in part because Drosophila have short generation times, so genetic differentiation happens quickly in terms of absolute time.
  • Squirrels – Antelope squirrels live in the desert Southwest, where one species may live on one side of a canyon while a closely related species lives on the other side. The two species look almost the same, and on a clear day they might even be able to see each other, but the canyon cuts them off from one another. Because gene flow was cut off, the two groups differentiated genetically to such an extent that they can no longer reproduce with each other, even if they are put back together.

One mechanism of speciation involves the establishment of a prezygotic or postzygotic barrier to gene flow. When a barrier to gene flow is established, the populations diverge genetically. Eventually, the genetic differentiation will lead to reproductive isolation, and the two groups will become separate species.

The Dobzhansky-Muller model is a simple model that explains how a single lineage can split into two reproductively isolated species. It explains the role of mutation on speciation. According to this model, something happens to subdivide a population into two isolated groups that evolve independently. Over evolutionary time, a different mutation may occur at different loci in each group. If these mutations convey an advantage, they will become fixed in the population over time. The mutant allele from one group may then be incompatible with the mutant allele in the other group, preventing the two groups from interbreeding.

Let's illustrate the Dobzhansky-Muller model with a diagram:

The Dobzhansky-Muller model also applies to major chromosomal rearrangements as well as point mutations that affect pairs of individual genes. For example, different species of bats of the genus Rhogeessa are thought to have evolved due to centric fusions of chromosomes—events in which two one-armed chromosomes have fused together at the centromere to form a single chromosome. Different groups with different patterns of centric fusion became reproductively isolated because their chromosomes could not line up during meiosis. This route to reproductive isolation is less common, and these bats are the best-known example of it.

Reproductive isolation tends to increase as species diverge genetically. That is, two groups of organisms are more likely to be classified as separate species if their DNA is vastly different than if their DNA is similar. Reproductive isolation can develop quickly over the course of a single generation, or it can develop over the course of millions of years. Therefore, while quantitative genetics has revealed that incompatible genes tend to accumulate slowly over time, even a small amount of genetic divergence in certain "key" genes can lead to reproductive isolation.

Sympatric Speciation

Sympatric speciation occurs when species arise from a population that remains connected. That is, there is no physical barrier between the subpopulations, and yet gene flow between them is still cut off. The word "sympatric" literally means "same country." This type of speciation is more common in plants than in animals.

For a long time, evolutionary biologists argued that speciation was essentially all allopatric. More and more, however, we are learning that sympatric speciation is also quite common, particularly in plants. Although sympatric speciation is generally more common in plants than in animals, Dr. Gillooly emphasized that recent research shows it is more common in animals than scientists once believed. Even so, it remains difficult to document and prove, because the populations must be shown to diverge without any physical barrier separating them.

Two causes of sympatric speciation include polyploidy and behavioral change:

  • Sympatric speciation by polyploidy (mostly in plants) occurs when a mistake during cell division results in an individual with cells that have more than one copy of the genome. New individuals that exhibit polyploidy are generally viable (meaning they can live and reproduce), but they are genetically isolated (meaning they cannot reproduce with "normal" diploid individuals). Moreover, increasing the size of the genome is usually associated with an increase in cell size, which has an effect on structure and function. These new individuals are often able to self-fertilize, allowing them to create offspring and thus propagate a new species. For example, imagine a population of diploid plants in which a mistake during meiosis produces a tetraploid individual. That tetraploid is immediately reproductively isolated from the diploid individuals around it. If its fitness is equal to or better than theirs, it will prosper, and a new species has formed essentially instantaneously. In fact, sympatric speciation by polyploidy is thought to be the predominant mode of speciation in plants.

Two forms of polyploidy are as follows:

  • Autopolyploidy exists when an individual has more than two sets of chromosomes, all from the same species.
  • Allopolyploidy exists when an individual has more than two copies of chromosomes, from different species.

Why are tetraploids reproductively isolated from diploids? During meiosis, a diploid individual produces haploid gametes, but a tetraploid individual produces diploid gametes. When a haploid gamete and a diploid gamete combine, the offspring is triploid (meaning it has three copies of each chromosome). Most of the gametes that a triploid produces are not viable because they carry an unbalanced set of chromosomes, so the triploid is essentially an evolutionary dead end.

The role of polyploidy in plant speciation is a very active area of research, much of it led by researchers at UF. For example, polyploids tend to be more common in colder climates and toward the poles than near the equator, for reasons that are not yet understood.

Sympatric speciation due to polyploidy is far more common in plants than in animals, but there are some cases of polyploidy in animals. For example, a tetraploid species of tree frog, Hyla versicolor, has appeared in the southeastern United States as a result of sympatric speciation by polyploidy. This tiny tree frog arose when two populations of a diploid tree frog came together and produced offspring with twice the usual number of chromosomes (4n instead of 2n). The tetraploid has been very successful and has spread, but it can no longer reproduce with its diploid ancestors because meiosis does not work properly between a tetraploid individual and a diploid individual.

Polyploidy is rare in animals overall, but Dr. Gillooly noted that amphibians are the major exception. In particular, salamanders and newts show relatively high rates of polyploidy compared to other animal groups, even though most documented cases still occur in plants.

  • Sympatric speciation by behavioral change (in animals) occurs when two parents make a "mistake" and accidentally go to a new breeding location. Their offspring are able to remember the place where they were born, and they return there to mate. This results in the formation of a new population in this place, and thus the propagation of a new species. More generally, the individuals of two subpopulations could still interact, but some change in behavior keeps them apart, and that eventually cuts off gene flow. Consider the following examples:
    • Apple maggot flies – These flies originally laid their eggs on hawthorn fruits. Then apples were introduced through agriculture, and some sensory change—perhaps, a change that impacted olfactory cells—caused some of these flies to come to prefer apples. A fly tends to eat the type of fruit that it is born on, so a food preference is passed on from generation to generation. Hawthorn bushes and apple trees can grow right next to each other, so there is no physical barrier between the two subgroups—flies that lay eggs on apples and flies that lay eggs on hawthorn fruits. Even so, this simple behavioral difference has cut off gene flow between them, and the two subgroups have diverged due to disruptive selection to the point of becoming reproductively isolated.
    • Sockeye salmon – Recall that sockeye salmon are anadromous, meaning they are born in freshwater, migrate to salt water, and then return to freshwater to reproduce. A sockeye is born in a small creek or river in a place like Alaska, spends about a year there, and then spends three or four years traveling around the Pacific basin before returning to the very same creek to reproduce and die. This behavior is hardwired: if the creek where a group of salmon was born is blocked off, the fish do not simply move to one of the creeks next door. Instead, they wait outside their home creek and die without reproducing. A professor at the University of Wisconsin discovered that the salmon know where to return based on smell. If salmon return to the wrong place and mate there, they can establish a new population of salmon that can become geographically isolated and differentiated from other kinds of salmon. In fact, it is thought that the six species of Pacific salmon arose in this way: a small number of individuals got confused and swam up the wrong creek, and their descendants kept returning there year after year.

      A researcher named Art Hasler discovered the mechanism underlying salmon homing behavior through a simple but clever experiment. While hiking, he realized he could smell a nearby creek before seeing it, which led him to hypothesize that salmon may also rely on smell to navigate back to their birthplace. He tested this by temporarily plugging the salmon's nasal passages with cotton, and the fish became completely disoriented. This experiment demonstrated that salmon use olfactory cues to locate the exact creek where they were born, which can lead to rapid behavioral divergence when individuals return to an unintended location.
    • Cichlid fishes – Cichlids are colorful fish that are popular in aquariums. Some African lakes contain several hundred species of cichlids, all living in the same lake. Over time, some cichlids began eating snails while others began eating something else, and some began using one part of the lake while others used another. In other words, the fish divided up behaviorally. Gene flow between the groups was cut off, the groups differentiated genetically, and new species formed.

Dr. Gillooly mentioned that reproductive isolation is involved in both sympatric speciation and allopatric speciation. In both of these patterns of speciation, a barrier to gene flow causes genetic differentiation, which leads to reproductive isolation.

Adaptive Radiation

Recall that Charles Darwin identified 13 or 14 different species of finches on the Galapagos Islands, all of which evolved from one common ancestor. This kind of relatively rapid evolution of many new, diverse species from a common ancestor under new environmental conditions is known as adaptive radiation. An adaptive radiation is a burst of speciation: a period of stasis is followed by an explosion of new species, often because organisms have been exposed to new environments. Keep in mind that "relatively rapid" still refers to evolutionary time, which can mean millions of years, and exactly how fast counts as rapid is not well-defined.

The following are some additional examples of adaptive radiation:

  • The evolution of cichlid fishes in African lakes.
  • The evolution of Drosophila fruit flies on the Hawaiian islands.
  • The evolution of Hawaiian silversword plants, which evolved in a similar fashion to the Drosophila fruit flies on the Hawaiian islands. In a relatively short amount of time, the silverswords differentiated tremendously, and there are now about 35 species.

Coevolution

Coevolution refers to the evolutionary influence that is shared between two species in the same environment. So far, we have been thinking about species in isolation, but many species interact heavily with other species, so the evolution of one species can be expected to affect the evolution of the other. For example, the acacia tree in Africa has evolved to provide a home for acacia ants in its swollen thorns, where small colonies of ants live, and to secrete a sugary substance from small nectaries for the ants to eat. In return, the ants have evolved to protect the tree from herbivores, such as elephants. If an elephant tries to eat the tree, the ants swarm up its trunk and aggravate it until it leaves. That is, these organisms have evolved together. (Dr. Todd Palmer, a professor at UF, has done significant research on the symbiotic relationship between acacia trees and ants.)

The idea that evolutionary pressures by one organism can influence the evolution of another gives rise to an "evolutionary arms race." The Red Queen hypothesis states that, because different species are coevolving, an individual species must continue to evolve if it wishes to survive. If two species interact intensively and one of them evolves, the other has to evolve just to keep up, and then the first has to evolve again just to keep up with it. This evolution does not increase the fitness of either species relative to the other; both are evolving simply to maintain the status quo. (Note that the term "red queen hypothesis" is based on a quote from the Red Queen in Lewis Carroll's Through the Looking Glass, who says, "It takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place.")

At the beginning of class, Dr. Gillooly briefly discussed one of the major unresolved patterns in evolutionary biology: the latitudinal gradient of biodiversity. This refers to the consistent trend in which species richness is highest in the tropics and declines toward the poles. Although many hypotheses attempt to explain this pattern, none fully accounts for it, and it remains an open question in the study of biodiversity. The Tempo of Speciation

Recall that, so far, we have discussed the role of evolution (descent with modification) and the effects of natural selection (differential reproductive success) on the process of evolution. We have also discussed how microevolution (a change in the frequencies of genotypes in a population) leads to macroevolution (the formation of new species). In this section, we will be considering the rate of evolution and extinction. That is: What controls the speed with which evolution progresses and species are formed?

Two Paradigms for the Tempo of Speciation

There are two different ideas as to how the tempo of speciation is controlled:

  • Gradualism – DNA mutates slowly, but gradually over time. Therefore, we will see a slow divergence of isolated populations over a period of millions and millions of years. This was the primary model used by evolutionary biologists until about 50 years ago.
  • Punctuated equilibrium – Species maintain prolonged periods of stasis, during which no major speciation occurs. Then, suddenly, these periods are interrupted by sudden episodes of speciation. (Note that, although this speciation is relatively rapid, it still takes several thousands of years.) This model was proposed around 1972 by Niles Eldredge, a paleontologist, and Stephen Jay Gould, an influential evolutionary biologist at Harvard.

Which of these mechanisms of evolution do we see on Earth? The answer is that evolution on Earth exhibits both gradualism and punctuated equilibrium. Fossil records show periods of slow evolution (gradualism), punctuated by periods of rapid evolution (punctuated equilibrium). Dr. Gillooly guessed that most evolutionary biologists today would say that punctuated equilibrium captures the nature of speciation better in general, but strong cases can be made for both models, depending on the species. Consider the following examples:

  • Cichlids (punctuated equilibrium) – Fossil records show that cichlid fish in Lake Victoria in Africa underwent rapid evolution, as 400 species evolved in just 12,000 years. This is almost instantaneous in terms of evolutionary time. For comparison, all of the Great Lakes combined contain only about 250 species of fish. Across the three major African lakes that contain cichlids, there are about 1,500 cichlid species with incredible morphological diversity. It is thought that differences in resource availability led these fish to occupy different parts of the lake and use different strategies. In particular, much of this speciation is thought to have come from subpopulations specializing on different diets within the same lake, which cut off gene flow and led to changes in the shape of the head that allow each species to exploit its diet. This is a good example of punctuated equilibrium. (Cichlids are a classic example of sympatric speciation, but a big adaptive radiation like this one is never exclusively sympatric or allopatric; hybridization and some allopatric speciation were probably involved as well.)
  • Horseshoe crabs (gradualism) – Fossil records show that horseshoe crabs, which are abundant in Florida, underwent a phenotypic stasis—and, thus, only gradual evolution—for 100 million years. This is a good example of gradualism. It also raises an interesting question: the environment has changed tremendously over that time, and yet the horseshoe crab phenotype has continued to be successful. (Interestingly, horseshoe crabs are medically important because their blue blood clots in the presence of bacterial contamination, making it invaluable for testing vaccines and other medical products for safety. Large ships harvest the crabs, their blood is extracted, and the crabs are then put back. Dr. Gillooly noted that it is the most expensive liquid on Earth—about $60,000 per gallon.)

Biologists are still working hard to characterize rates of speciation and to compare them across species. For example, Dr. Gillooly showed a 2021 study that estimated rates of speciation in vertebrates (amphibians, birds, fish, mammals, and reptiles). The different groups overlap a good deal, but within vertebrates the rates vary by orders of magnitude, depending on environmental conditions.

When we think about speciation in a phylogenetic context, there are two possible scenarios, called anagenesis and cladogenesis. Anagenesis occurs when gradual changes accumulate within a lineage until the ancestral species has changed so much that it is considered a new species. Because the ancestral species is effectively gone, anagenesis does not add a species or increase biodiversity. In contrast, cladogenesis occurs when a new species branches off from an ancestral species that itself persists, forming a clade. Because both species now exist, cladogenesis increases biodiversity by one species.

What Controls the Rate of Speciation?

A combination of the following factors controls the rate of speciation:

  • Rates of mutation – The faster the mutation of an organism's genome, the faster the rate of speciation. Recall that mutation is the ultimate source of genetic diversity.
  • Rates of selection – Rates of selection can be controlled by competition among the species for food, mates, or space, and by the amount of available habitat. They can also be impacted by generation time. For example, bacteria have shorter generation times than desert tortoises. All other things the same, you would expect bacteria to evolve more rapidly than desert tortoises.
  • Rates of environmental change – Periods of extreme environmental change intensify natural selection by altering resource availability, habitat structure, and competitive pressures. For example, as we will see later, mass extinctions are generally followed by rapid speciation because survivors encounter new ecological opportunities.

Dr. Gillooly's research focuses on the impact of environmental change—particularly changes in temperatures—on rates of evolution. He has found that mutation rates in ectotherms increase exponentially with environmental temperature because higher temperatures accelerate rates of cell division. His research predicts that, all else equal, organisms living at 20°C experience roughly 300% higher mutation rates than organisms living at 10°C.

Which of these factors matters most? Dr. Gillooly said that it depends, and that biologists are still struggling to understand it. We can generalize to some degree: organisms with shorter generation times, and organisms that experience a high rate of environmental change, are more likely to undergo speciation. However, we are not yet able to look at different species and make quantitative predictions about their rates of speciation.

Exaptation

Speciation does not occur from out of nowhere. That is, natural selection must start with structures that are already in place.

An exaptation occurs when a structure evolves for one purpose and comes to be used for another. Once an evolutionary change occurs, it may serve a different function when introduced to a new context. Moreover, it can be further modified to serve a different function. This can lead to an evolutionary novelty.

Two examples of exaptations are as follows:

  • Wings in Archaeopteryx – Archaeopteryx, a Jurassic bird about the size of a crow, shows how new structures evolve from pre-existing ones. Early birds already possessed feathered limbs, and research suggests they used these limbs to run up trees and gain lift while escaping predators. Over time, these structures became increasingly modified, eventually supporting true powered flight. The key idea is that wings did not appear suddenly; they arose through stepwise modifications to structures that already existed.
  • Moray eel jaws – Moray eels have an extra set of jaws that they use to pull prey down their throats. These jaws did not evolve from scratch; instead, they originated from modified gill arches that were repurposed over evolutionary time. This illustrates the broader principle that new traits must arise from pre-existing structures and cannot appear fully formed.

Causes of Rapid Speciation

The following factors can cause rapid speciation:

  • Key genetic processes that change the organism's phenotype but maintain its function. For example, polyploidy occurs when gamete fusion accidentally results in a doubling or tripling of the genome. Polyploidy prevents the new organism (with multiple copies of the genome) from mating with individuals with only one copy of the genome, thus leading to the rapid propagation of a new species. This phenomenon is especially prevalent in plants.
  • Key developmental processes that change the organism's phenotype but maintain function. Alteration of the genes that affect the structure of the organism can lead to rapid speciation. For example, homeotic (Hox) genes control the early organization of body structure during development, and even small mutations can produce dramatic phenotypic changes. Dr. Gillooly illustrated this with a classic example in flies: a single mutation in a Hox gene can cause legs to grow where the antennae should be. Although most such mutations are detrimental, rare changes to key developmental regulators can generate major shifts in phenotype and contribute to rapid speciation.

Also, changes in developmental timing or the sequence of events (referred to as heterochrony) can lead to significant phenotypic changes. To picture the difference, imagine that an organism develops in ten steps and that each step takes one day. A change in sequence would mean that the steps occur in a different order (for example, step 5 occurring before step 3). A change in timing would mean that the order stays the same but some of the steps take much more or much less time than one day. Two kinds of heterochrony are as follows:

  • Allometric heterochrony is a change in the rate of growth or development. For example, most breeds of domesticated dogs reach their maximum size, and reach maturity, at an earlier point in time than wolves, which is why adult dogs are smaller than adult wolves. This change in the rate of growth or development has led to a significant phenotypic change that allowed domesticated dogs to diverge from wolves.
  • Sequence heterochrony is a change in the time at which a structure appears. Consider, for example, the Axolotl—a large Mexican salamander that, unlike other salamanders, does not internalize its gills as it goes from the juvenile stage to the adult stage. (This is an example of neoteny, which occurs when adults retain juvenile traits; it is a type of sequence heterochrony.) This kind of delayed development has allowed the Axolotl to live in aquatic environments, leading to vast differences in behavior and anatomy. This salamander is studied at UF because it can regenerate its limbs. It also has an enormous genome, and no one really knows why.

Some scientists believe that there is evidence of sequence heterochrony and neoteny in humans, as well.

The theory is that humans have retained the "baby faces" of other primates, as human faces are flatter than the adult faces of most primates. For example, a juvenile chimpanzee's skull looks very different from an adult chimpanzee's skull, and the shape of the human skull is more like that of the juvenile. Dr. Gillooly cautioned that this idea has been proposed, but it is hard to pin down whether it is true.

The Tempo of Extinction

As speciation adds new species to the Earth's collection over time, extinction removes species from the Earth. Just like speciation, extinction is a continuous process that constantly occurs at some background rate due to chance and natural selection, is accelerated by environmental change, and can occur in rapid bursts.

To understand the dynamics that establish biodiversity on earth, you must understand the dynamic interplay between speciation and extinction. Scientists have estimated that the current extinction rate is higher than ever in history, in part because humans are modifying the environment over large portions of the Earth. The current rate is about 100 to 1,000 times higher than the background extinction rates that we are able to discern from the available fossil record, and it continues to accelerate. Scientists project that the extinction rate in the future could reach about 10,000 times the background rate. Dr. Gillooly noted that there is a lot of misinformation about extinction in the popular press. It is true that extinction has always happened, but not at the rate we see today.

Although correlation does not always imply causation, Dr. Gillooly emphasized that in this case the relationship between rising extinction rates and the exponential growth of the human population is not merely correlational. Extensive research shows that human-driven habitat destruction, climate change, and resource use are directly causing the accelerated extinction patterns observed today. The specific causes vary with the organisms in question, but they include loss of habitat, development, human population growth, and pollution.

The exponential increase in the number of extinction events in the last 250 years has corresponded with an exponential increase in the number of humans on the planet. There is little doubt that human activity is driving a large share of the extinctions that are occurring today. As a result, scientists call the current geological age the anthropocene—the period during which human activity is the dominant influence on climate and the environment.

Extinction threatens some organisms more than others. For example, amphibians are experiencing one of the most severe biodiversity crises on Earth. Dr. Gillooly noted that about 43% of amphibian species are threatened. Changes in water temperature and pH and the loss of habitat all play a role, but the biggest cause is the chytrid fungus, a skin infection that has spread all over the world and is driving rapid, global declines. We do not yet know how to control it. He emphasized that amphibians could go completely extinct within our lifetimes, as researchers working in remote parts of Africa, far from any people, have returned after a single year to find entire populations wiped out. In addition, insect populations are on the decline. A 2018 study showed a stunning 76% decline in the biomass of insects in Germany over about 30 years, and later studies have shown the same thing. Insects are around us all day long, and yet no one had noticed, which shows that major losses of diversity can happen without our being aware of them.

EXAM TIP: Mass Extinctions

Dr. Gillooly emphasized that it is important to know the Cretaceous and Permian mass extinctions.

Rapid bursts of extinction (where more than 50% of species are lost) are known as mass extinction events. There have been five mass extinction events in the Earth's history, though many believe that we are currently in the sixth major mass extinction event in the Earth's history. Two of the most severe mass extinction events in the Earth's history were the following:

  • The Cretaceous mass extinction, which wiped out about 71% of all species 65 million years ago. This is the mass extinction that wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs. The avian (flying) dinosaurs survived. No one can say for sure why, but most of them were small, so they did not need much food, and it is thought that they could still find things like seeds on the ground even after the forests died.
  • The Permian mass extinction wiped out about 83% of all genera and 97% of all aquatic genera 250 million years ago. Only a handful of animals remained, such as Lystrosaurus, a weird herbivore the size of a pig, which was the most common terrestrial vertebrate in its day. Lystrosaurus is sometimes called a "proto-mammal" because it is thought to be the ancestral species that led to modern mammals, and its fossils have been found all over the world. No one knows for certain why the Lystrosaurus lineage was one of the only tetrapods to survive the Permian mass extinction. Dr. Gillooly mentioned one possibility, which is that Lystrosaurus may have been able to burrow underground. He added that an animal the size of a pig is not what he would have guessed would survive and prosper after a mass extinction.

What caused these mass extinctions? Scientists are still uncertain about what caused the Permian mass extinction, in part because it occurred 250 million years ago. Dr. Gillooly said that it is really hard to know. One idea is that massive volcanic eruptions occurred and that methane gas, possibly released from the deep sea, took over the atmosphere.

In contrast, there is a consensus about the Cretaceous mass extinction: it is thought to have been triggered by a massive meteor impact in the Yucatan Peninsula. Satellite imaging has revealed a 112-mile-wide impact crater in this region, which you can still visit today. How could a single meteor wipe out 71% of the species on the planet? Dr. Gillooly explained that the impact clouded the skies with dust, so sunlight could not get through. Without sunlight, primary production stopped: plants could not grow, forests and plankton died, and then the animals that eat them died as well.

Note that periods of mass extinction are almost always followed by periods of punctuated speciation because the post-mass-extinction environment favors the propagation of new speciation. For example, the age of the mammals followed the Cretaceous mass extinction.

You can think of a "tree of life" as a snapshot of the interaction between speciation and extinction, which has led to the divergence of different groups of organisms over time. Most phylogenetic trees, including the ones that we discussed earlier in this course, describe the relationships among species that are alive today and show organisms in a hierarchical relationship. This leads many people to incorrectly presume that certain species are "more evolved" than others.
