Introduction to Projects

  • Project: a series of related tasks aimed at a major output, with a distinct start, a distinct end, and a defined budget. Research (a vaccine, software) counts as well as construction.
  • Project management has three steps:
StepManager's tasksUseful tools
PlanningSet time, cost, and performance objectives; organize the team; build the work breakdown structure (tasks → subtasks → activities, small enough to estimate)Time/cost estimates, budgets, engineering diagrams, cash flow charts
SchedulingSequence activities; assign people; schedule resources and deliverablesCPM, PERT, Gantt charts, milestone charts
ControllingMonitor cost, resources, and quality; revise plans; shift resourcesBudgets, delayed-activity and slack-activity reports
  • Project management triangle: time, cost, and performance scope trade off against each other.

Project Organization

  • A temporary, cross-functional team. Works best for a specific goal and deadline; unfamiliar, complex, interrelated work; many functions involved; and important but temporary projects.

Project Managers

  • Project manager: coordinates and monitors the project; responsible for quality, schedule, budget, and the people on it.

EXAM TIP: Client Satisfaction

Client satisfaction is the most important measure of a project's success, ahead of schedule and budget.

Project Management Software

  • Microsoft Project, Primavera, Agile tools (smaller projects), or internal software tied to accounting.
  • Ethics questions (PMI code): gifts from contractors, false expense or status reports, pressure to cut quality to stay on schedule.

Project Scheduling Techniques (Overview)

  • Gantt charts, CPM, and PERT show where each activity fits, set precedence (what must come first), force realistic time and cost estimates, and expose bottlenecks.

Gantt Charts

  • Gantt chart: one bar per activity, from its start to its finish; overlapping bars are activities running at the same time.

PERT and CPM

  • CPM (Critical Path Method): activity times are known with fair certainty.
  • PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique): activity times vary, so it can give the probability of finishing by a date.
  • Six steps: define the activities; set precedence; draw the network; estimate times; compute the critical path; use the network to schedule and control.

Activity Network Conventions

  • Activity-on-node (AON): nodes are activities. Activity-on-arrow (AOA): arrows are activities, and may need dashed dummy activities.

EXAM TIP: Activity-on-Node Method

The exam uses activity-on-node networks only.

CPM Example: Milwaukee Paper Manufacturing

The EPA gives Milwaukee Paper 16 weeks to install pollution control equipment.

Step 1: Define the project and break it down into specific activities.

  • Eight activities, A through H (named in the table under Step 4).

Step 2: Determine the precedence relationships among the different activities.

  • A and B start the project; C follows A; D follows A and B; E and F follow C; G follows D and E; H follows F and G.

Step 3: Draw the network that connects all of the activities.

PERT and Variability in Activity Times

  • Each activity's time follows a right-skewed beta distribution with three estimates: optimistic (a), most likely (m), pessimistic (b).
  • Project completion time follows a normal distribution; activity times are assumed independent.

\( t = \frac{a + 4m + b}{6} \qquad v = \left(\frac{b - a}{6}\right)^2 \qquad \mu = \Sigma\, t_{\text{critical}} \qquad \sigma_p^2 = \Sigma\, v_{\text{critical}} \)

EXAM TIP: Critical Path Calculations

Add expected times and variances along the critical path only. You cannot add standard deviations: square each to a variance, add, then take the square root.

Review of Normal Distribution

  • Symmetric: 50% on each side of the mean; about 68%, 95%, and 99.7% fall within ±1, ±2, and ±3 standard deviations.

\( z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} = \frac{\text{Due date} - \text{Expected date}}{\sigma_p} \)

  • Critical path A → C → E → G → H: μ = 2 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 2 = 15 weeks; σ² = 0.11 + 0.11 + 1.00 + 1.78 + 0.11 = 3.11; σ = √3.11 = 1.76 weeks.
  • By the 16-week deadline: z = (16 − 15) ÷ 1.76 = 0.57 → P = 0.71566 (Excel: =NORM.S.DIST(0.57, TRUE)).
  • For a 99% chance: z = 2.33 (P = 0.9901), so x = zσ + μ = (2.33)(1.76) + 15 = 19.10 weeks.

Variability of Completion Time for Non-Critical Paths

  • A non-critical activity can still delay the project if its variability is large next to its slack.

Conclusion

  • Expected completion 15 weeks; a 71.57% chance of meeting the 16-week deadline.
  • A, C, E, G, and H are critical; B, D, and F have slack.

Project Crashing

  • Crashing: shortening the project by spending more on some activities, because it is behind schedule, the deadline moved up, or finishing early saves fixed costs.
  • Crash only critical-path activities that can actually be shortened, at the lowest cost.

\( \text{Crash cost per period} = \frac{\text{Crash cost} - \text{Normal cost}}{\text{Normal time} - \text{Crash time}} \)

  1. Find each activity's crash cost per period.
  2. Find the critical path(s) with current times.
  3. One critical path: crash its cheapest crashable activity. Several: crash an activity common to all of them, or one on each.
  4. Update the times; stop at the due date, otherwise return to step 2.

Crashing the Project by Multiple Time Periods

  • Crash one period at a time and redo both passes, since the critical path can change.
  • 14 → 13 weeks: A is used up. Options: G ($1,500) or H ($3,000), common to both paths; or one on each path: C + D or E + D ($2,000), C + B or E + B ($3,000). Crash G; both paths stay critical at 13 weeks.

Key Takeaways

  • Client satisfaction is the top measure of project success.
  • Forward pass: ES = largest EF of the predecessors; EF = ES + duration.
  • Backward pass: LF = smallest LS of the followers; LS = LF − duration.
  • Slack = LS − ES; zero-slack activities form the critical path, the longest path and the shortest project time (15 weeks in the example).
  • PERT: t = (a + 4m + b)/6 and v = ((b − a)/6)²; add t and v along the critical path only, never standard deviations.
  • z = (due date − μ)/σp; 16 weeks gives a 71.57% chance, and 99% needs 19.10 weeks.
  • Crash the cheapest critical activity one period at a time, and re-check the critical paths after each.
