The Nature of Aggregate Planning

  • Aggregate planning (in industry, sales and production (S&P) planning): planning intermediate-term production in aggregate units (how many cars, not how many blue sedans), because totals forecast better than individual items.
  • Goal: meet forecast demand at minimum cost over the planning period by adjusting production rates, labor, inventory, overtime, and subcontracting.
  • Requirements: a logical aggregate unit; an aggregate demand forecast; a way to determine costs; a model combining forecasts and costs.
  • The plan works like a budget authorization and feeds the master production schedule and MRP, which break it down by product.

Aggregate Planning Strategies

  • Capacity options change supply; demand options change demand. Each comes in opposite pairs (hire or lay off; build inventory or back-order).

Capacity Options

OptionDrawbacks

Methods of Aggregate Planning

  • No single option is likely to be optimal, partly because company policies limit each one, so planners combine several.
  • Chase strategy: match output to each period's demand (common, often the only option, in services).
  • Level strategy: uniform daily production, with inventory or idle time as the buffer.
  • Mixed strategy: a combination, usually best; finding it takes trial and error.

EXAM TIP: Work Many Practice Problems

The routine problems hide small rules (inventory carried in, charging for a workforce change at the start). The exam has no wrinkle that is not also in the practice questions.

Graphical Approaches (Trial and Error)

  • Graphical methods compare demand with capacity a few variables at a time; easy, but not guaranteed optimal.
  • Steps: demand per period; capacity (regular, overtime, subcontracting); labor, hiring, layoff, and holding costs; company policies; then compare alternative plans' total costs.

Costs: carrying $5 per unit per month; subcontracting $20 per unit; labor $80 per day per worker (1.6 labor-hours per unit); raising the daily rate $300 per unit; lowering it $600 per unit.

Method 1: Pure Level Strategy

  • 6,200 ÷ 124 = 50 units per day with 10 workers. Ending inventory 1,850 × $5 = $9,250; labor 10 × $80 × 124 = $99,200. Total $108,450.

Method 2: Level Strategy (Production at the Minimum Requirement and Use of Subcontracting)

  • 38 units per day (March's rate) with 7.6 workers: 7.6 × $80 × 124 = $75,392. 6,200 − 4,712 = 1,488 units subcontracted × $20 = $29,760. Total $105,152, the lowest of the three.

Method 3: Pure Chase Strategy (Using Hiring and Layoffs)

  • Production $99,200 + hiring $9,000 + layoffs $9,600. Total $117,800.
  • Other plans (overtime, for one) were not tried, and nonmonetary costs such as layoffs' effect on morale also count.

Transportation Method of Linear Programming

  • An optimizing method: supply (by period and source) in the rows, demand periods in the columns, and the unit cost in each cell.

Example: Transportation Method

A tire maker has regular time at $40, overtime at $50, and subcontracting at $70 per tire; 100 tires in beginning inventory; $2 per tire per month carrying cost; and no back-ordering.

Setting Up and Solving the Table

  • Capacity 2,680 + 100 inventory exceeds demand of 2,550, so a dummy column takes the 230 unused units at $0.
  • Each cell costs the source's price plus $2 for every month held; cells that would need back-ordering are blocked.
  • Fill each month's demand from the cheapest source with units left, earlier months' production included. Step through it to see the table built and filled one source at a time.

The Transportation Method, Step by Step

Press Next to build the table and the plan one step at a time.

  • The plan: March: 100 from inventory + 700 regular. April: 700 regular, 50 overtime, 150 subcontracted in April, plus 50 overtime and 50 subcontracted in March and held a month. May: 700 regular + 50 overtime.
  • Total cost = 100(0) + 700(40) + 50(52) + 50(72) + 700(40) + 50(50) + 150(70) + 700(40) + 50(50) = $105,700.
  • The method gives a starting plan; the stepping-stone method or Excel's Solver can confirm or improve it.

EXAM TIP: Reading Aggregate Production Plans

A question about the period of production (made, ordered, delivered) reads a row; one about the period of demand (sold, used to meet demand) reads a column. April demand met by subcontracting: 50 + 150 = 200 (April column). March overtime paid for: 50 (March overtime row).

Management Coefficients Model

  • Management coefficients model: a regression on managers' past decisions; objective and consistent, but assumes past trends continue and past decisions were good.

Key Takeaways

  • Aggregate planning covers 3 to 18 months, in aggregate units, at minimum cost.
  • Capacity options change supply; demand options shift or reshape demand.
  • In the shingle example, the level-at-minimum plan with subcontracting is cheapest at $105,152.
  • Transportation method: carrying cost grows with every month held; no back-ordering blocks later production meeting earlier demand; a dummy column balances supply and demand.
  • The tire plan costs $105,700.
  • Production period → row; demand period → column.
