Question 1 · Labor Markets

Once restaurants were allowed to reopen, many of their workers feared catching COVID-19 from diners. Because of that fear:

  1. Does the demand curve for restaurant workers move right, move left, or stay put?
  2. Does the supply curve of restaurant workers move right, move left, or stay put?
  3. What happens to the equilibrium wage and to the number of restaurant workers employed?

Solution

Suggested responses to this short-answer question are as follows:

  1. The concern about catching COVID-19 is a factor that affects the supply curve for restaurant workers, not the demand curve.
  2. The supply curve for restaurant workers shifted to the left, as fewer restaurant workers were willing to work at each wage rate.
  3. A leftward shift of the supply curve for restaurant workers caused an increase in the "price" of their labor (the wage rate) and a decrease in the quantity of restaurant workers employed.

Question 2 · Monopolistic Competition

Knight's dog-vacuuming service is a firm in monopolistic competition that cleans 50 dogs each week. It charges $40, its marginal cost is $45, and its average total cost is $45. Which describes the firm?

  1. It may be maximizing profit, and it is taking an economic loss
  2. It is certainly not maximizing profit, yet it earns an economic profit
  3. It is certainly maximizing profit, and it earns a normal profit
  4. It is certainly not maximizing profit, and it earns a normal profit
  5. None of these

Solution

(e) Like all firms, Knight's dog vacuuming company will maximize its economic profit by producing at a point where marginal cost is equal to marginal revenue. We are told that Knight's price is $40 that his marginal cost is $45. A monopoly firm's marginal revenue curve lies below its demand curve, so its marginal revenue will always be less than its price. Therefore, we know that Knight's marginal revenue is less than $40, so his marginal revenue could not possibly be equal to his marginal cost. Therefore, we can conclude that Knight is definitely not maximizing his profit.

The rules for economic profit, normal profit, and economic loss are as follows:

  • If P > ATC, the firm earns an economic profit.
  • If P = ATC, the firm earns only a normal profit.
  • If P < ATC, the firm earns an economic loss.

Here, Knight's price is $40 and his average total cost is $45. Because P < ATC, Knight must be earning an economic loss.

Therefore, we can conclude that Knight is definitely not maximizing his profit, and he is earning an economic loss.

Question 3 · Monopoly

Which statements hold for a monopoly in the long run?

  1. It may earn an economic profit.
  2. Its price equals its marginal revenue.
  3. It always produces the output that minimizes its average total cost.
  1. i alone
  2. ii alone
  3. iii alone
  4. ii and iii only
  5. None of these

Solution

(a) A monopoly firm can earn an economic profit in the long run because there are insurmountable barriers to entry. It is not true that a monopoly firm's price equals its marginal revenue because the firm's marginal revenue curve lies below the demand curve. (This statement would be true for a perfectly competitive firm, not a monopoly firm.) It is also not true that the firm also produces the quantity that minimizes its average total cost.

Question 4 · Oligopoly

The firms in an oligopoly have formed a cartel. When is the industry's total profit lowest?

  1. When every firm keeps to the agreement
  2. When one firm breaks the agreement and the rest keep to it
  3. When every firm breaks the agreement
  4. When the firms act together as a single monopoly

Solution

(c) Firms enter a collusive agreement to increase their profits. If all firms cheat on the collusive agreement, it would be as if there was no collusive agreement in the first place. Therefore, total industry profits would be smallest when all firms cheat on the agreement.

Question 5 · Monopoly

Regulators require a natural monopoly to charge a price equal to its average total cost. The firm then

  1. takes an economic loss
  2. earns a normal profit, but total surplus is not maximized
  3. earns an economic profit
  4. maximizes total surplus, but earns less than a normal profit
  5. earns a normal profit and maximizes total surplus

Solution

(b) The average cost pricing rule says that (1) The firm must set P=ATC; and (2) The firm's quantity must be equal to the amount customers will buy. Therefore, under an average cost pricing rule, the natural monopoly will produce at the point where the demand curve and the ATC curve intersect.

The advantage of average cost pricing regulation is that the firm will earn a normal profit. (Stated differently, it will earn zero economic profit—profit over and above a normal profit. It will also not incur an economic loss.) This is true because the price will equal the average total cost. The disadvantage of average cost pricing regulation is that the firm will not be producing the efficient amount. We know this because the marginal benefit curve (demand) is not equal to the marginal cost at this point. The firm will be underproducing, and a deadweight loss occurs.

Question 6 · Externalities

A good's marginal external cost is equal to its

  1. marginal social cost less its marginal social benefit
  2. marginal social benefit less its marginal social cost
  3. marginal social benefit less its marginal external benefit
  4. marginal private cost less its marginal private benefit
  5. None of these

Solution

(e) The marginal social cost is equal to the marginal private cost plus the marginal external cost. That is,

\(MSC = MC + MEC\)

Therefore, when a market is at equilibrium, the marginal external cost equals the difference between the marginal social cost and the marginal private cost:

\(MEC = MSC - MC\)

Question 10 · Externalities

An unregulated competitive market produces a good that creates an external benefit. Why is the outcome inefficient?

  1. People consume too much of the good
  2. People consume too little of the good
  3. The market price is too high
  4. The good costs too much to produce

Solution

(b) The efficient quantity occurs at the intersection of the MSB curve and the MSC curve, and the actual quantity produced occurs at the intersection of the MB curve and MSC curve. When there is an external benefit from consuming a good, the marginal social benefit (MSB) curve lies above the marginal private benefit (MB) curve. As a result, the quantity actually produced is less than the efficient quantity, meaning that too little of the good is produced.

Question 11 · Oligopoly

Target and Walmart are each deciding whether to cut their prices. The table gives each firm's economic profit for every combination of choices (T is Target's profit, W is Walmart's). Which firm, if either, has a dominant strategy?

  1. Walmart does; Target does not
  2. Target does; Walmart does not
  3. Both firms do
  4. Neither firm does
  5. None of these

Solution

(d) Editor's note: The answer key that was posted online indicates that (B) is the correct answer, but we believe that (D) is the correct answer to this question.

This is a payoff matrix question, which is one of the more difficult questions that you could receive on an exam. Following is a methodical way for you to approach this question and questions like it. The payoff matrix is the one shown with the question.

First of all, you should recognize that a firm has a dominant strategy when it is best off taking one course of action, no matter what the other firm does. A firm does not necessarily have to have a dominant strategy. In this game, both Target and Walmart could have dominant strategies, one could have a dominant strategy and not the other, or neither of them could have a dominant strategy. We have to work the matrix to solve the problem.

Step 1: If Target sets low prices, what is Walmart's best option?

For the purposes of this question, only consider the column that corresponds to Target setting low prices.

If Target sets low prices, Walmart makes $3 million by setting low prices and $9 million by setting high prices. Walmart will set high prices.

Step 2: If Target sets high prices, what is Walmart's best option?

For the purposes of this question, only consider the column that corresponds to Target setting high prices.

If Target sets high prices, Walmart makes $18 million by setting low prices and $2 million by setting high prices. Walmart will set low prices.

Because Walmart's best strategy depends on whether Target sets low or high prices, Walmart does not have a dominant strategy.

Step 3: If Walmart sets low prices, what is Target's best option?

For the purposes of this question, only consider the row that corresponds to Walmart setting low prices.

If Walmart sets low prices, Target makes $3 million by setting low prices and $6 million by setting high prices. Target will set high prices.

Step 4: If Walmart sets high prices, what is Target's best option?

For the purposes of this question, only consider the row that corresponds to Walmart setting high prices.

If Walmart sets high prices, Target makes $8 million by setting low prices and $7 million by setting high prices. Target will set low prices.

Because Target's best strategy depends on whether Walmart sets low or high prices, Target does not have a dominant strategy.

In this question, neither Target nor Walmart have dominant strategies.

Question 12 · Elasticity

How price elastic is the demand for one particular luxury car model, such as the Cadillac Escalade, likely to be?

  1. Elastic, though not perfectly elastic
  2. Inelastic, though not perfectly inelastic
  3. Zero
  4. Infinite

Solution

(a) The key determinant of the price elasticity of demand is the number of substitutes there are for the good. The more substitutes, the more elastic the demand for the good. There are a lot of substitutes for a Cadillac Escalade, so the demand for such a car is likely to be elastic. However, it isn't going to be perfectly elastic because a slight increase in price wouldn't cause the quantity demanded to go to zero. Stated differently, the demand curve for a Cadillac Escalade is likely to be pretty flat, but not completely flat.

Question 13 · Inequality

Computerization raises the demand for workers with a lot of human capital and lowers the demand for workers with little. Its effect on the distribution of income is to

  1. make it more equal
  2. leave it unchanged
  3. make it less equal
  4. change it in a direction that cannot be determined

Solution

(c) Individuals with a lot of human capital already earn more income than those who have little human capital. If computerization increases the demand for labor with a lot of human capital and decreases the demand for labor with little human capital, the wages paid to people with a lot of human capital will rise and the wages paid to people with little human capital will fall. Question 14 · Supply and Demand

Zucchini turns out to help prevent tooth decay, and at the same time zucchini pickers win a higher wage. What happens to the price and quantity of zucchini?

  1. Price rises and quantity increases
  2. Price rises and quantity decreases
  3. Price falls; the quantity change is ambiguous
  4. Price rises; the quantity change is ambiguous
  5. The price change is ambiguous; quantity decreases

Solution

(d) This is a question that involves a shift of both the supply and demand curve. Note that you will almost definitely get one of these questions on an exam. When you get this kind of a question, you should follow the following steps:

  1. Draw two supply and demand curves and label everything
  2. Determine which curve shifts for each factor that changes
  3. Determine the direction of each shift
  4. On the first diagram, show a huge shift in demand and a small shift in supply. On the second diagram, show a huge shift in supply and a small shift in demand.

Here, we are told that zucchini is found to help prevent tooth decay. This would cause a positive change in preferences that would shift the demand curve for zucchini to the right. Simultaneously, we are told that zucchini pickers negotiate a higher wage. This is an increase in the cost of producing zucchini, which causes the supply curve to shift to the left.

On one diagram, show a large rightward shift of the demand curve and a small leftward shift of the supply curve. You will find that the equilibrium price increases and that the equilibrium quantity increases. On the other diagram, show a large leftward shift of the supply curve and a small rightward shift of the demand curve. You will find that the equilibrium price increases and the equilibrium quantity decreases.

In both cases the price increased, so we can tell unambiguously that the price will increase. However, we don't know what will happen to the quantity: it could increase, decrease, or stay the same depending on the relative magnitude of the shifts of the supply and demand curves.

Question 15 · Labor Markets

When the wage rate goes up, which way do the income effect and the substitution effect push the number of hours people choose to work?

  1. Income effect toward more hours; substitution effect toward fewer
  2. Both toward more hours
  3. Both toward fewer hours
  4. Income effect toward fewer hours; substitution effect toward more

Solution

(d) There are two effects that shape the labor supply curve.

  • Substitution Effect - As the wage increases, the opportunity cost of spending time not working (leisure) goes up. Thus, people will respond by working more. This is the idea that if people make more money, they are more willing to work.
  • Income Effect - As the wage rate increases, income increases and the demand for normal goods and services increases. Because time not working (leisure time) is a normal good, the supply of labor goes down.

Therefore, as the wage rate rises, the income effect influences workers to work fewer hours, and the substitution effect influences workers to work more hours.

Question 16 · Perfect Competition

A perfectly competitive market starts in long-run equilibrium, and then demand for its product rises for good. Compared with before, the new long-run equilibrium has

  1. fewer firms
  2. more firms
  3. the same number of firms
  4. a different number of firms, but whether more or fewer cannot be determined

Solution

(b) A permanent increase in demand for a good produced by a perfectly competitive industry will cause the price of that good to rise. As the price rises, in the short run, P>ATC and each individual firm can earn an economic profit. Because there are no barriers to entry into a perfectly competitive industry, in the long run, firms will enter the industry, causing the industry supply curve to shift to the right and the price to fall. As the price falls, the economic profit earned by each existing firm falls as well. Therefore, the new long-run equilibrium has more firms producing a higher quantity of output than before, but each individual firm will earn only a normal profit.

Question 18 · International Trade

Which groups does a tariff harm?

  1. Domestic producers
  2. Domestic consumers
  3. The domestic government
  1. i alone
  2. ii alone
  3. iii alone
  4. i and iii
  5. i, ii and iii

Solution

(b) A tariff is a tax on imported goods. Tariffs increase the price of the good imported, which reduces the quantity of imported goods. As a result, consumer surplus falls and domestic producer surplus increases. Therefore, a tariff harms consumers, who have to pay more for the good than if the tariff had not been imposed. Tariffs benefit domestic producers (who get to charge a higher price and sell more of their goods), and they benefit the government (which generates tariff revenue). However, the harm to domestic consumers outweighs the benefit to domestic producers and the government, which means that tariffs create a deadweight loss.

Question 22 · Perfect Competition

Every firm in a perfectly competitive market is taking an economic loss. If some of the firms leave the market, then

  1. the market supply curve shifts right
  2. the product's price falls
  3. the remaining firms' losses shrink
  4. the remaining firms' losses grow
  5. None of these

Solution

(c) There are no barriers to entry or exit in a perfectly competitive industry. If existing firms are earning an economic loss (P < ATC), some firms will exit the industry. This will cause the industry supply curve to shift to the left, which will increase the market price. Individual perfectly competitive firms are price-takers, so the leftward shift of the industry supply curve will also increase the price that each individual firm can charge. As a result, the economic losses of the remaining firms will decrease. In the long run, P = ATC and the firms will earn a normal profit.

Question 28 · Elasticity

The price of salt drops 15 percent, and the quantity of salt demanded rises 5 percent. What is the price elasticity of demand for salt?

  1. 0.33
  2. 3.0
  3. 0.75
  4. 75.0

Solution

(a) This question requires you to be able to apply the basic formula for elasticity. The price elasticity of demand is a measure of how responsive quantity demanded is to changes in price. It is defined as the absolute value of the percentage change in demand divided by the percentage change in price. Here, we know that the percentage change in quantity is 5% and the percentage change in price is -15%. Thus, we can calculate the elasticity of demand:

\( E = \left|\frac{\%\Delta Q_D}{\%\Delta P}\right| \)

\( E = \left|\frac{5\%}{-15\%}\right| = \underline{\mathbf{0.33}}\)

Question 4 · Inequality

A country's Lorenz curve moves further away from the line of equality. Which of these must be true of the country's income?

  1. It has fallen.
  2. It has risen.
  3. It is shared less equally.
  4. It is shared more equally.
  1. ii alone
  2. iii alone
  3. i and iii
  4. ii and iv
  5. None of these

Solution

(b) Economists use the Lorenz curve to measure distribution of income. A Lorenz curve plots the cumulative percent of a nation's income against the cumulative percent of its households. The straight diagonal line is called the Line of Equality, or the 45° line. This represents a situation where income distribution is exactly equal. The farther a Lorenz curve is from this line, the less equally distributed the nation's income.

Note that the Lorenz curve tells us only about the distribution of income in a country; it does not tell us about the absolute level of income. Therefore, if the Lorenz curve moves farther away from the line of equality, we can tell that income has become less equally distributed, but we don't know whether the nation's income has risen, fallen, or stayed the same.

If the size of the substitution effect is greater than the size of the income effect, an increase in the wage rate will increase the quantity of labor supplied. (This corresponds with the upward-sloping portion of the labor supply curve.)

Question 27 · Government Intervention

The equilibrium wage for low-skilled labor is $13.25. The federal government lifts the minimum wage from $8.25 to $15.00 an hour. The result is

  1. less unemployment
  2. more unemployment
  3. a shortage of low-skilled labor
  4. neither a shortage nor a surplus of low-skilled labor

Solution

(b) The minimum wage is a price floor below which it is illegal to pay workers. Like all price floors, the minimum wage creates a surplus. In the case of the minimum wage, we call this surplus unemployment. The higher the price floor—that is, the higher the minimum wage—the greater the resulting unemployment. Therefore, the government's action of increasing the minimum wage results in an increase in unemployment.

In this case, the $8.25 per hour minimum wage was not binding, meaning it did not have any impact on the market, because it was set below the equilibrium wage. When the government increased the minimum wage to $15 per hour, the minimum wage became binding, and it created a surplus of labor in the market. That is, it resulted in unemployment.

Question 28 · Supply and Demand

A new fertilizer lets farmers grow more barley, while research finds that eating barley can cause severe headaches. Supply and demand shift by the same amount. What happens to the equilibrium price and quantity of barley?

  1. Price falls; the quantity change is ambiguous
  2. The price change is ambiguous; quantity decreases
  3. Price falls; quantity does not change
  4. Price falls and quantity increases
  5. Price does not change; the quantity change is ambiguous

Solution

(c) This question involves a simultaneous shift of the supply and demand curves. There is no need to draw two supply and demand diagrams, however, because we are told that the magnitude of the supply curve shift is equal to the magnitude of the demand curve shift.

A new kind of fertilizer that allows farmers to produce more barley than before is a technological advance in the production of barley—a factor that shifts the supply of barley to the right. Research that shows that eating barley can cause severe headaches would cause a negative change in consumer preferences for barley—a factor that shifts the demand curve to the left.

When the supply curve shifts to the right, the equilibrium price decreases and the equilibrium quantity increases. When the demand curve shifts to the left, the equilibrium price decreases and the equilibrium quantity decreases. Both of these cause the equilibrium price to decrease, so we know that the price will decrease. We're told that the magnitude of the shifts of the curves are the same, so the opposite impacts on the quantity cancel each other out. As a result, we can conclude that the equilibrium price of barley decreases, but the equilibrium quantity does not change.

Question 29 · Oligopoly

Target and Walmart are each deciding whether to cut their prices. The table gives each firm's economic profit for every combination of choices (T is Target's profit, W is Walmart's). Which firm, if either, has a dominant strategy?

  1. Walmart does; Target does not
  2. Target does; Walmart does not
  3. Both firms do
  4. Neither firm does

Solution

(c) This is a payoff matrix question, which is one of the more difficult questions that you could receive on an exam. Following is a methodical way for you to approach this question and questions like it. The payoff matrix is the one shown with the question.

First of all, you should recognize that a firm has a dominant strategy when it is best off taking one course of action, no matter what the other firm does. A firm does not necessarily have to have a dominant strategy. In this game, both Target and Walmart could have dominant strategies, one could have a dominant strategy and not the other, or neither of them could have a dominant strategy. We have to work the matrix to solve the problem.

Step 1: If Target sets low prices, what is Walmart's best option?

For the purposes of this question, only consider the column that corresponds to Target setting low prices.

If Target sets low prices, Walmart makes $7 million by setting low prices and $11 million by setting high prices. Walmart will set high prices.

Step 2: If Target sets high prices, what is Walmart's best option?

For the purposes of this question, only consider the column that corresponds to Target setting high prices.

If Target sets high prices, Walmart makes $3 million by setting low prices and $9 million by setting high prices. Walmart will set high prices.

Because Walmart's best strategy does not depend on whether Target sets low or high prices, Walmart has a dominant strategy.

Step 3: If Walmart sets low prices, what is Target's best option?

For the purposes of this question, only consider the row that corresponds to Walmart setting low prices.

If Walmart sets low prices, Target makes $7 million by setting low prices and $6 million by setting high prices. Target will set low prices.

Step 4: If Walmart does not lower prices, what is Target's best option?

For the purposes of this question, only consider the row that corresponds to Walmart setting high prices.

If Walmart sets high prices, Target makes $10 million by setting low prices and $5 million by setting high prices prices. Target will set low prices.

Because Target's best strategy does not depend on whether Walmart sets low or high prices, Target has a dominant strategy.

Thus, both Walmart and Target have dominant strategies.
